# CMI's institutional context

*CMI's research spans development economics, political science, anthropology, and law across politically sensitive contexts in the Global South. This breadth generates uniquely valuable data, but also distinctive challenges: protecting participants in conflict zones, navigating fragmented regulatory frameworks, and ensuring that partnerships do not reproduce the power asymmetries they study. Understanding these dynamics is essential before making decisions about how data should be managed, shared, or withheld.*

## A diverse data landscape

CMI organises its research across ten thematic clusters, covering areas such as conflict and humanitarianism, democracy and governance, migration, global health, and climate and natural resources.[^1] In 2024, the institute ran 92 externally funded projects.[^2] This portfolio generates highly diverse data types, each carrying its own management challenges:

| Data category | Examples | Key challenge |
|---|---|---|
| Quantitative datasets | RCT results, household surveys, yield measurements | De-identification, cross-border transfer |
| Qualitative records | Interview transcripts, focus groups, life histories | Contextual identifiability, relational harm |
| Fieldwork materials | Ethnographic notes, photographs, audio/video | Participant and researcher safety |
| Policy and legal documents | Reports, contracts, regulatory analyses | Confidentiality, client privilege |
| Geospatial data | GPS coordinates, settlement maps, resource mapping | Location-based re-identification |

The combination of a Global South focus and work on sensitive topics (authoritarianism, conflict, forced migration, corruption) means that much of this data carries elevated risks. Participant identifiability can arise not only from direct identifiers but from contextual variables, geospatial clues, or narrative fragments. A single interview transcript from a diaspora community or a set of field notes from a conflict zone may put people at risk in ways that standard anonymisation cannot fully resolve.

## Sensitivity and regulatory complexity

Research in conflict-affected and authoritarian contexts creates risks of retaliatory harm if participants or researchers are identified. These risks are compounded by regulatory fragmentation: studies document discordant data protection regimes across EU, African, and other national frameworks, with limited Research Ethics Committee capacity for multi-jurisdictional review.[^3]

The EU's General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) can, however, serve as a human-rights-based safeguard where other protections are insufficient. The GDPR embeds privacy as a fundamental right, imposing standards of lawful, transparent, and proportionate processing.[^4] For CMI, this means GDPR compliance is not merely a bureaucratic obligation; it can function as a baseline of protection for participants in jurisdictions where domestic safeguards are weak or absent.

!!! info "GDPR Article 89: a research-specific framework"
    Article 89 permits processing of sensitive personal data for scientific research purposes, provided that appropriate safeguards are in place. These include data minimisation, pseudonymisation, and privacy by design.[^5] This gives CMI a legal basis for working with sensitive data while protecting participants.

## Partnership dynamics and power

Multi-national research collaborations frequently centralise data infrastructure in Global North institutions while partners in the Global South concentrate on data collection. This reproduces inequitable divisions of labour that affect dataset ownership, publication decisions, and credit.[^6] Guidance on equitable partnerships exists but remains unevenly applied.[^7]

These asymmetries are not only operational but epistemic. Community-oriented perspectives on data sovereignty challenge assumptions of frictionless openness and Northern custodianship.[^6] Indigenous and African communitarian ethics, including frameworks such as Ubuntu and the CARE Principles, position communities as rights-bearing actors rather than passive data sources, emphasising collective decision-making and benefit sharing.[^8] Recent work extends CARE beyond its Indigenous origins, arguing that its principles apply equally in humanitarian crises and conflict-affected settings where data about displacement or political violence can be weaponised against those it describes.[^9] For the philosophical arguments underpinning these frameworks, see [Philosophical and epistemic foundations of data sharing](philosophical-foundations-of-data-sharing.md).

Moving beyond extractive fieldwork requires embedding reciprocal arrangements into research design. Bothello and Bonfim propose 'research infusion' as an alternative: a mode of knowledge co-creation grounded in respect, reciprocity, and reflexivity, where communities actively shape research processes and outcomes.[^10] In practice, this means negotiated authorship, participatory analysis, or shared custodianship of knowledge. For qualitative data in particular, where re-use risks misinterpretation and relational harm, controlled access, contextualised metadata, and community-led governance may be more appropriate than default openness.[^11]

## Opportunities for rebalancing

The same structural features that create challenges also offer opportunities to recalibrate what counts as high-value data and to model more equitable research practice.

### Data as representation

Data is not a neutral mirror of reality; it is a medium of representation that amplifies some voices while silencing others. For CMI, operating in politically sensitive and fragile contexts, this means adopting an explicit role as a voice-amplifier for underrepresented perspectives. Hybrid dissemination strategies, where controlled-access arrangements coexist with public-facing outputs, allow community voices to be heard while safeguarding those at risk.[^12]

### Hybrid governance beyond open and closed

CMI's work across diverse regulatory environments positions its researchers to develop governance models that move beyond simplistic open/closed binaries. Documentation strategies for multi-site comparative projects can preserve local meanings and contextual specificity while enabling systematic cross-site analysis.[^7] In politically sensitive settings, differentiated access regimes allow each dataset to be governed according to its actual risk profile:

- **Open access**: materials available without restriction.
- **Controlled access**: released under specific conditions (e.g., approved research use, data use agreement).
- **Metadata only**: description published, underlying data withheld.
- **Local retention**: data held exclusively by local partners or communities.

### Innovation under constraint

The demands of managing sensitive data in conflict-affected or authoritarian contexts can drive methodological innovation. When participant safety, surveillance, and political repression are constant risks, standard approaches to openness and storage are insufficient. Techniques developed under these pressures (secure metadata-only publishing, privacy-by-design workflows, community-controlled archives, layered access permissions, minimal data transfers) have wider application across CMI's entire portfolio. What begins as a necessity in fragile settings can become a model for good practice elsewhere.

## What this means for practice

CMI's mission requires high-quality, findable, and interpretable data aligned with EU and funder expectations of transparency. At the same time, commissioned work may require strict confidentiality. Impact depends on strategic visibility: balancing openness for peer and policy engagement with confidentiality for participant safety and partner trust.

Portfolio diversity enables CMI to draw connections across contexts and reach different audiences, but it demands governance frameworks that treat data management not as a technical add-on but as central to engaged, equitable research.
