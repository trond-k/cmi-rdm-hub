# Stag 1 - Frame: design and conceptualise

*Good data management starts before data collection, before the funding application, and before the Data Management Plan. It starts when you first think concretely about what data your research questions require. The decisions you make (or postpone) at this stage cascade through the entire lifecycle. Getting them right early prevents costly redesign later.*

## Match data to research questions

The first question is deceptively simple: what data do you actually need to answer your research questions? Not what data would be interesting to have, or what data you could collect while you are in the field, but what is necessary and sufficient.

This distinction matters because scope creep in data collection is expensive and creates downstream obligations. Every dataset you collect must be stored, documented, protected, and eventually either preserved or destroyed. Collecting data you do not need wastes resources and, where personal data is involved, may put you in conflict with the GDPR's data minimisation principle.

Start by listing your research questions and, for each one, identifying the minimum data needed to address it. Be specific about the unit of analysis, the variables or themes, and the level of detail required. If a question can be answered at the district level, you may not need household-level data. If a question concerns policy frameworks, you may need documents rather than interviews.

!!! tip "Write it down before it drifts"
    Even at the concept stage, a brief note linking each research question to its data requirements helps the whole team stay aligned. This does not need to be formal; a table in the concept note is enough. It becomes the seed for your [data inventory](data-inventory.md).

## Check what already exists

Before committing to primary data collection, survey the landscape of existing data. Can your research questions be answered, wholly or partly, with secondary data? Publicly available datasets, government statistics, administrative records, previous project data held by CMI or partner institutions, and data in repositories such as the [UK Data Service](https://ukdataservice.ac.uk/), [ICPSR](https://www.icpsr.umich.edu/), or the [World Bank Microdata Library](https://microdata.worldbank.org/) may cover some of your needs.

Reusing existing data is not a compromise. It reduces cost, avoids burdening participants with duplicate collection, and can strengthen your analysis by enabling comparison with established datasets. It also raises its own questions: Is the existing data well documented? Are its terms of access compatible with your project? Is it recent enough? Does it cover the population or geography you need?

Where existing data partially addresses your questions, a mixed strategy (secondary data supplemented by targeted primary collection) is often more efficient than starting from scratch.

## Assess feasibility

Identifying the right data is one thing; being able to collect it is another. Feasibility has several dimensions, and all of them are worth confronting early:

- **Practical.** Can you physically reach the field sites? Do you have the equipment, software, and personnel to collect the data you have specified? Are there seasonal, logistical, or infrastructural constraints?
- **Financial.** Can the data collection be done within a realistic budget? Transcription, translation, secure storage, and data management all cost money. If you cannot budget for proper handling, you may need to narrow the scope.
- **Ethical.** Can the data be collected without exposing participants to unacceptable risk? For research in conflict-affected or authoritarian contexts, this is not a formality. Consider whether informed consent is genuinely possible, whether participation could be observed by hostile actors, and whether the data itself could be used to identify or harm individuals if it were disclosed.
- **Legal.** Does the data involve personal information subject to the GDPR or other data protection frameworks? Will cross-border data transfers be needed, and if so, under what legal basis? Are there contractual or intellectual property constraints on data you plan to acquire from third parties?

If the answer to any of these is 'probably not', it is better to redesign now than to discover the problem mid-project.

## Get an early sense of data types and formats

You do not need a detailed technical specification at this stage, but you should have a clear picture of the kinds of data your project will generate. The management challenges differ substantially:

| Data type | Examples | What to think about early |
|---|---|---|
| Quantitative | Survey responses, experimental measurements, administrative statistics | Variable definitions, coding schemes, file formats (CSV, SPSS, Stata) |
| Qualitative | Interview transcripts, focus group recordings, field notes, life histories | Transcription and translation needs, storage of audio and video, contextual identifiability |
| Documents and text | Policy reports, legal instruments, grey literature, archival materials | Selection criteria, source tracking, copyright and access terms |
| Geospatial | GPS coordinates, settlement maps, remote sensing imagery | Re-identification risk from location data, software requirements, file size |
| Audiovisual | Photographs, video recordings, oral histories | Consent for recording and future use, storage volume, format longevity |

Many CMI projects work with several of these simultaneously. Recognising the mix early helps you plan storage, anticipate processing needs, and budget accurately.

## Screen for sensitivity

Data sensitivity is not binary. A dataset that seems straightforward in one context can become dangerous in another. At the FRAME stage, you are not conducting a full risk assessment; you are flagging the areas that will need careful attention as the project develops.

Ask yourself:

- Does the data contain personal information, whether directly identifying (names, ID numbers) or potentially identifying through context (a specific role in a small community, a distinctive set of demographic characteristics)?
- Does it fall into the GDPR's special categories: racial or ethnic origin, political opinions, religious beliefs, health data, trade union membership, genetic or biometric data?
- Could disclosure cause harm to participants, their families, or their communities, whether through legal repercussions, social stigma, political retaliation, or physical danger?
- Does the data involve indigenous or community knowledge where governance rights may apply?
- Are there commercial confidentiality or client privilege concerns, particularly in commissioned research?

!!! warning "Sensitivity can change"
    A dataset that appears low-risk at the design stage may become sensitive if political circumstances shift, if a conflict escalates, or if new information makes participants more identifiable than anticipated. Build in the expectation that sensitivity classifications may need to be revised during the project. See the [data inventory](data-inventory.md) for how to track these changes.

For CMI projects in conflict-affected, authoritarian, or politically volatile settings, the default assumption should be that data is sensitive until demonstrated otherwise. This does not mean you cannot collect it. It means you plan for protection from the outset rather than retrofitting safeguards when a problem emerges.

## Sketch a preliminary data inventory

By the end of this stage, you should have enough information to draft a preliminary [data inventory](data-inventory.md): a list of the datasets you expect to create or acquire, with a rough indication of their type, source, likely sensitivity, and purpose. This is not a formal deliverable; it is a thinking tool. It forces you to be concrete about what your project will actually involve in data terms, and it provides the foundation that the [FUND](fund.md) and [PLAN](plan.md) stages will build on.

Even a brief inventory at this stage often surfaces questions that the concept note left implicit. How many interviews are you actually planning? What happens to the recordings after transcription? Who will hold the survey data during fieldwork? These are easier to answer now than six months into the project.
