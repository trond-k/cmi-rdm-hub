# FUND: data in funding applications

*The funding application is where data management commitments become binding. What you promise here, on openness, sharing, storage, and ethical safeguards, shapes what you must deliver. Getting the data sections right is not just about ticking boxes; it is about securing the resources you will need and avoiding commitments you cannot keep.*

## Why this matters at proposal stage

Funders increasingly treat data management as a core component of research design, not an administrative afterthought. The Research Council of Norway (RCN), Horizon Europe, the European Research Council (ERC), and Norad all expect applicants to demonstrate that they have thought seriously about what data the project will generate, how it will be managed, and how it will be made available. Reviewers notice when data sections are vague or boilerplate.

This is also the stage where mismatches become expensive. If you promise open access to all project data but your fieldwork involves sensitive interviews in a conflict zone, you will spend the rest of the project trying to reconcile an impossible commitment. If you underbudget for data management, you will either cut corners or redirect funds from other activities. The goal is to make commitments that are ambitious but realistic, and to secure the resources to honour them.

## Articulate a data management strategy

Your proposal should describe, in concrete terms, how data will be managed across the project. This is not the full DMP (that comes at the [PLAN](lifecycle-3-plan.md) stage, usually after the grant is awarded), but it should demonstrate that you understand what is involved. Cover at minimum:

- What types of data the project will generate or acquire, and in what approximate volume.
- How data will be stored and protected during the project.
- What metadata standards and documentation practices you will follow.
- How and where data will be made available after the project, including any restrictions.

Draw on the groundwork from the [FRAME](lifecycle-1-frame.md) stage. If you have already sketched a preliminary [data inventory](data-inventory.md), this section writes itself. If you have not, this is a strong incentive to do so.

## Address ethics and sensitivity

Most funders expect the proposal to address the ethical dimensions of data handling. For CMI projects, this typically means explaining how you will:

- Protect participants, particularly in conflict-affected, authoritarian, or otherwise sensitive contexts.
- Handle personal data in compliance with the GDPR and any applicable local frameworks. For detailed guidance, see [GDPR and legal compliance](CROSS-gdpr-and-legal-compliance.md).
- Manage risks of re-identification, especially where contextual variables, geospatial data, or small populations make standard de-identification insufficient.
- Respect the rights and interests of communities from whom data originate, including questions of data sovereignty and governance. See [CMI's institutional context](cmi-institutional-context.md) and [Foundations of knowledge and data sharing](foundations-of-data-sharing.md).

Do not overcommit to openness and then rely on exceptions later. If you know from the outset that certain datasets cannot be shared openly, say so in the proposal and explain why. Funders understand legitimate restrictions; what they do not appreciate is discovering them at the reporting stage.

## Know what your funder requires

Funder requirements vary in specificity and scope, and they change. The following gives a broad orientation, but always check the current call documentation.

=== "Research Council of Norway (RCN)"

    RCN requires a DMP for all projects that generate or use research data. The plan should follow RCN's template or an equivalent (such as the [Science Europe guidance](https://www.scienceeurope.org/our-priorities/open-science/)). RCN expects data to be made as open as possible and as closed as necessary. A DMP is typically due shortly after the project start, not at proposal submission, but the application should outline the data management approach.

=== "Horizon Europe"

    Horizon Europe requires a DMP as a deliverable, normally due within the first six months. The proposal must describe the data management approach, including FAIR compliance, in the relevant work package. Open access to research data is the default under Horizon Europe, with justified opt-outs for specific datasets. Data management costs are eligible.

=== "ERC"

    ERC grants fall under Horizon Europe rules. A DMP is required as a deliverable. The proposal should address open access and data management in line with Horizon Europe's open science requirements.

=== "Norad and commissioned work"

    Commissioned projects may have specific data ownership and confidentiality clauses that override default openness. Review the contract terms carefully. Where the commissioning body retains ownership or imposes restrictions on publication, document these constraints clearly and plan data management accordingly.

!!! info "Last reviewed"
    Funder requirements change regularly. This overview was last reviewed on 26 March 2026. Verify against the current call documentation before submitting.

## Justify restrictions honestly

The default expectation across most funders is that data should be as open as possible. But 'as open as possible' is not the same as 'open without qualification'. Where restrictions are necessary, state them clearly and provide the rationale:

- **Participant protection.** Data from vulnerable populations, conflict zones, or authoritarian contexts may carry risks of re-identification or retaliation that cannot be mitigated through anonymisation alone.
- **Legal constraints.** GDPR requirements, contractual obligations, or intellectual property arrangements may limit what can be shared and when.
- **Commercial confidentiality.** Commissioned research may involve proprietary information or client privilege.
- **Embargoes.** Some funders or publishers allow a period of exclusive use before data must be made available.

For each restricted dataset, indicate what will be shared (metadata, documentation, summary statistics, anonymised subsets) and under what conditions. Funders are far more likely to accept a well-reasoned restriction than an unexplained one.

## Budget for data management

Data management costs money, and the funding application is where you secure it. Common cost items include:

| Category | Examples |
|---|---|
| Personnel | Data manager or coordinator time, research assistant effort for documentation and cleaning |
| Storage and infrastructure | Secure cloud storage, encrypted file-sharing services, specialised environments for sensitive data |
| Software and tools | Qualitative analysis software licences, survey platforms, transcription services |
| Transcription and translation | Professional transcription of interviews, translation of instruments or outputs |
| Repository and preservation | Deposit fees (where applicable), data curation, long-term preservation costs |
| Training and capacity building | Data management training for team members and partners |

Do not assume these costs are trivial or that the institution will absorb them. If you cannot fund proper data management, you may need to reduce the data you collect. An underbudgeted project that generates poorly managed data serves nobody.

!!! tip "Check what CMI provides"
    CMI's existing infrastructure (Microsoft 365 E5, institutional repository access, advisory support) covers some data management needs at no direct cost to the project. Factor these in-kind contributions into your budget narrative so that reviewers see the full picture, and so that you know what genuinely requires additional funding.

## Make the case for data as an output

Funders increasingly recognise data as a first-class research output, not just a by-product of publications. Your proposal can strengthen the case for funding by articulating the reuse value of your data:

- Who might reuse it? Other researchers, policymakers, journalists, international organisations?
- What makes it distinctive? Does it cover an underrepresented population, a hard-to-access context, or a novel combination of variables?
- How will it be documented and described so that others can interpret and use it without your involvement?

This is not about inflating the significance of your data. It is about being explicit that well-managed, well-documented data has value beyond the original project, and that the investment in managing it properly will pay off.

## Refine the data inventory

At this stage, the [data inventory](data-inventory.md) moves from the preliminary sketch of the FRAME stage to a more structured version. Map each anticipated dataset explicitly to a research question or work package. Increase the specificity of format, volume, and sensitivity estimates. The inventory now supports both the data management strategy and the ethics narrative in the proposal, and it provides the basis for realistic budgeting. It is not yet a formal deliverable; that comes at the [PLAN](lifecycle-3-plan.md) stage.
