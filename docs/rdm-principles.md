# CMI Data Management Principles

## An Institutional Framework for Responsible Research Data Management

-----

### Purpose

This document establishes a shared set of principles to guide data management across all CMI research activities. These principles are not prescriptive rules but a common foundation — a reference point for project teams as they develop context-specific Data Management Plans (DMPs) and make practical decisions about how data is collected, stored, shared, and preserved.

The principles reflect CMI’s commitment to research that is rigorous, ethical, and responsive to the conditions in which it takes place. They draw on established frameworks (including FAIR, do-no-harm, and data protection norms) while recognising that good data management is always shaped by context.

Individual projects should interpret and operationalise these principles in ways that are appropriate to their specific research settings, populations, disciplines, and risk profiles. What matters is that the reasoning behind those choices is transparent and documented.

-----

### 1. FAIR as a Framework, Not a Formula

**Data should be findable, accessible, interoperable, and reusable — but always in proportion to what is ethically and practically appropriate.**

The FAIR principles (Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable) provide a valuable orientation for data management. CMI endorses them as a framework for improving the discoverability, quality, and long-term value of research data. However, FAIR is not treated as an absolute standard to be maximised in every case.

In practice, this means:

- **Findability** is pursued through clear metadata and documentation, even when the underlying data cannot be openly shared.
- **Accessibility** is calibrated to context — some data may be accessible only under controlled conditions, to specific users, or after an embargo period.
- **Interoperability** involves using clear formats and standards where possible, while acknowledging that qualitative, multilingual, or context-dependent data may require interpretive guidance rather than technical standardisation alone.
- **Reusability** depends on adequate documentation, but also on whether reuse is ethically warranted and whether sufficient context can be provided to support responsible secondary use.

Projects should document how they interpret and apply each FAIR dimension, and where they deliberately limit openness, explain the reasoning.

-----

### 2. Transparency and Accountability

**Research processes and data decisions should be visible and justified, so that others can understand what was done and why.**

Transparency at CMI is understood as a practice of accountability — making the logic of data management decisions legible to colleagues, funders, reviewers, and (where appropriate) research participants and publics. It is not reducible to open data publication.

This principle asks that projects:

- Document data management choices clearly, including decisions *not* to share or to restrict access.
- Maintain traceable records of data processing, transformation, and anonymisation steps.
- Be forthcoming about limitations, uncertainties, and trade-offs in their data practices.
- Communicate openly with funders and partners about what data will and will not be made available, and on what grounds.

The guiding stance is **“as open as possible, as closed as necessary”** — sharing findings and (where appropriate) data in ways that serve accountability and scholarly exchange, while recognising that openness is a means to those ends, not an end in itself.

-----

### 3. Do No Harm

**No action, output, or decision arising from CMI research should expose participants, communities, or other affected parties to foreseeable harm.**

This is a foundational commitment. It applies throughout the data lifecycle — from the design of data collection instruments to the long-term storage or disposal of datasets — and it takes precedence when it comes into tension with other principles (including openness and reuse).

“Harm” is understood broadly to include physical, psychological, social, legal, economic, and reputational risks, and extends beyond direct participants to families, communities, and associated individuals. Risk is assessed not only at the point of data collection but in light of how data might be used, linked, or reinterpreted over time.

Projects working in high-risk settings — including conflict-affected areas, authoritarian contexts, or with vulnerable populations — should give this principle particular weight, recognising that even seemingly routine data may carry risks in such environments.

-----

### 4. Data Minimisation

**Collect and retain only the data that is necessary for the research objectives.**

Minimisation is both a privacy safeguard and a practical discipline. It reduces the volume of sensitive material that must be protected, limits the potential consequences of a data breach, and encourages researchers to be intentional about what they gather and why.

This principle applies to:

- The scope of data collected (avoiding “just in case” collection of sensitive details).
- The retention of data over time (disposing of or de-identifying data that is no longer needed).
- The granularity of data shared or archived (providing only the level of detail required for the intended use).

Projects should be able to justify the necessity of each category of data they collect, particularly when working with vulnerable populations or sensitive topics.

-----

### 5. Proportionality

**Security measures and access restrictions should be matched to the actual sensitivity and risk level of the data.**

Not all data carry the same risks, and not all risks call for the same response. Proportionality asks that projects assess the sensitivity of their data realistically and apply protections that are adequate but not excessive.

This means:

- Highly sensitive data (e.g., identifiable information from conflict-affected settings, data on political activity, health records) receive the strongest protections — encryption, restricted access, secure storage, and careful transfer protocols.
- Lower-risk data (e.g., published sources, aggregated statistics, fully anonymised datasets) may be managed with lighter controls, facilitating collaboration and reuse.
- Security measures are reviewed periodically and adjusted as risk profiles change over the course of a project.

Over-restriction can be as problematic as under-protection: it impedes legitimate research collaboration, wastes resources, and can obscure the reasoning behind data management choices.

-----

### 6. Access Control and Least Privilege

**Access to research data is granted on the basis of demonstrated need and defined roles, and limited to the minimum required for each function.**

This principle protects both participants and the integrity of the research process. It recognises that not everyone involved in a project needs the same level of access to the same data, and that access decisions should be deliberate rather than default.

In practice:

- Access permissions are role-based and documented.
- Access is reviewed when roles change (e.g., when team members leave a project or new collaborators join).
- Particular care is taken with data that could identify individuals, especially in sensitive research contexts.
- Where data is shared externally (e.g., with partners or for replication), the scope and conditions of access are clearly agreed in advance.

-----

### 7. Shared Responsibility

**Ethical and secure data management is a collective obligation, not the sole domain of a designated data manager or PI.**

While specific roles within a project carry distinct duties (the principal investigator, data manager, field researchers, IT support), the underlying commitment to responsible data handling is shared by everyone who touches the data.

This principle implies:

- All team members receive appropriate guidance or training on data management expectations relevant to their role.
- Responsibilities are clearly assigned and documented, but ethical vigilance is expected of all.
- Partner institutions and collaborators are held to equivalent standards, formalised through data sharing agreements or equivalent arrangements where appropriate.
- A culture of mutual accountability is fostered, in which raising concerns about data practices is welcomed rather than discouraged.

-----

### 8. Context Sensitivity

**Data management practices must be informed by the specific social, political, cultural, and disciplinary context of the research.**

Good data management is not context-neutral. What constitutes “sensitive” data, appropriate consent, or adequate anonymisation varies across settings, populations, and disciplines. A practice that is routine in one context may be harmful or meaningless in another.

This principle asks project teams to:

- Assess risks and design data management practices with specific attention to the conditions in which the research takes place — including political dynamics, community tensions, legal frameworks, and cultural norms around information.
- Avoid applying generic templates uncritically; instead, adapt institutional guidance to the realities of the research environment.
- Recognise that context may change over time (e.g., a political situation may deteriorate, or previously non-sensitive data may become sensitive), and plan for review and adaptation accordingly.

For research in conflict-affected or politically sensitive settings, this principle overlaps with and reinforces the do-no-harm commitment.

-----

### 9. Informed and Meaningful Consent

**Participants should understand, to the fullest extent practicable, what data is being collected, how it will be used, and what control they retain over it.**

Consent is not merely a procedural requirement but an ongoing ethical relationship. CMI recognises that truly informed consent can be difficult to achieve — particularly in contexts of power asymmetry, low literacy, cultural difference, or political constraint — and expects projects to engage seriously with these challenges rather than treating consent as a checkbox.

This includes:

- Providing clear, accessible, and honest information about data practices, in languages and formats appropriate to the participants.
- Being transparent about the limits of anonymisation and the potential for re-identification.
- Allowing for withdrawal of consent where feasible, and explaining clearly where it is not.
- Adapting consent processes to the research context (e.g., oral consent, community consultation, staged consent for longitudinal studies).
- Revisiting consent when the scope or use of data changes significantly.

-----

### 10. Data Quality and Integrity

**Data should be collected, processed, and documented to a standard that supports the credibility and reproducibility of the research.**

Responsible data management is not only about ethics and security — it is also about the quality and reliability of the data itself. Poorly documented, inconsistently processed, or inadequately preserved data undermines both the immediate research and any future reuse.

This principle encompasses:

- Consistent and well-documented procedures for data collection, coding, transcription, and transformation.
- Version control and audit trails for datasets that undergo processing or revision.
- Adequate metadata to enable others to understand the provenance, scope, and limitations of a dataset.
- Quality checks appropriate to the data type and discipline.

-----

### 11. Responsible Data Retention and Disposal

**Data should be retained for as long as it serves a justified purpose and disposed of securely when it does not.**

Not all data should be kept indefinitely. Retention decisions should balance the potential value of data for future research, verification, or accountability against the risks of continued storage — particularly for sensitive or identifiable data.

Projects should:

- Define retention periods at the planning stage, in line with funder requirements, legal obligations, and ethical considerations.
- Establish clear procedures for secure disposal or anonymisation at the end of the retention period.
- Distinguish between data that should be archived for long-term preservation and data that should be deleted after the project concludes.
- Document retention and disposal decisions as part of the DMP.

-----

### 12. Legal and Regulatory Compliance

**All data management practices must comply with applicable legal and regulatory requirements, including data protection law, institutional policy, and funder mandates — but legal compliance alone does not guarantee ethical adequacy.**

CMI operates across multiple jurisdictions and regulatory environments. Projects are expected to identify and comply with the relevant legal frameworks — including, but not limited to, the EU General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR), national data protection and cybercrime legislation in countries where research is conducted, and funder-specific data policies.

Where legal requirements conflict with ethical principles (e.g., where a legal framework permits or compels data sharing that would endanger participants), the more protective standard should generally prevail, with the reasoning clearly documented.

Importantly, legal compliance must be understood in conjunction with Principle 13 below, which addresses situations in which national laws themselves may pose risks to research participants.

-----

### 13. Navigating Adverse Legal Environments

**Where national laws or regulatory frameworks may function as instruments of surveillance, control, or repression, CMI prioritises the safety and rights of participants over formal legal compliance with those frameworks.**

This principle responds to a reality that CMI cannot afford to overlook. A growing number of countries — including some in which CMI conducts or supports research — have enacted data protection, cybercrime, or digital governance legislation that, in design or implementation, serves purposes fundamentally at odds with the protection of individuals. These laws may:

- **Compel data localisation** in ways that place research data within the reach of security services, rather than protecting it. In several jurisdictions, requirements to store data on domestic servers are widely understood to facilitate state access rather than enhance privacy. This is documented across multiple African and Middle Eastern contexts, where localisation mandates have been adopted without corresponding safeguards against government intrusion.
- **Criminalise broadly defined online conduct** — such as “offensive communication,” “false information,” or vaguely worded “cyberstalking” — under cybercrime statutes that are routinely used to target journalists, activists, and researchers. Such provisions exist in the legislation of numerous countries in Eastern, Central, and West Africa, among other regions.
- **Frame the state as protector of privacy** while exempting state agencies from meaningful oversight. Some governments have enacted data protection laws that regulate private actors but grant expansive surveillance powers to the state, presenting an outward appearance of rights protection while consolidating control over information flows.
- **Require reporting or disclosure obligations** that could expose participants — for example, mandatory reporting of “cybercrimes” that may encompass legitimate research activities or the communications of participants in sensitive contexts.
- **Restrict encryption or secure communication** in ways that undermine the ability of researchers to protect data in transit or at rest.

#### Implications for CMI Research

When operating in such environments, CMI projects should:

1. **Conduct a legal environment assessment** as part of project planning, evaluating not only the text of applicable laws but their implementation in practice — including patterns of enforcement, government access to data, and the treatment of researchers, journalists, and civil society actors.
1. **Apply the do-no-harm principle as the overriding standard.** Where compliance with a national law would foreseeably expose participants, communities, or researchers to risk of harm — whether through compelled data disclosure, forced localisation, or weakened encryption — the project should prioritise participant safety. This may mean declining to store data in-country, using secure communication channels regardless of local restrictions, or limiting the collection of data that could be requisitioned.
1. **Avoid data localisation in jurisdictions where domestic storage increases risk.** Where a government’s track record suggests that locally stored data is vulnerable to state access without adequate judicial oversight, projects should consider storing data in jurisdictions with stronger protections — even if this conflicts with local data localisation mandates. Such decisions should be documented and, where possible, discussed with legal counsel and ethics advisors.
1. **Treat formal legal compliance as necessary but not sufficient.** A law that is nominally about “data protection” or “cybersecurity” may in practice function as an enabler of repression. CMI does not accept that compliance with such laws discharges the institution’s ethical responsibilities. Legal analysis should always be accompanied by a contextual assessment of how laws operate in practice.
1. **Maintain operational security awareness.** In high-risk environments, projects should consider the security of their own communications and data practices — including the use of encrypted channels, awareness of interception risks, and careful management of metadata — as integral to responsible data management.
1. **Engage proactively with funders and ethics bodies.** Where a project determines that participant safety requires departing from strict legal compliance in a given jurisdiction, this reasoning should be documented transparently and discussed with relevant funders, institutional ethics committees, and legal advisors. The goal is to ensure that such decisions are principled, defensible, and collectively supported.
1. **Monitor and reassess.** Legal and political environments can change rapidly. A jurisdiction that is relatively stable at the outset of a project may become more repressive over its course. Projects should build in mechanisms for periodic reassessment and be prepared to adapt their data management practices accordingly.

#### A Note on Complexity

This principle does not imply a blanket disregard for national law, nor does it suggest that all data localisation or cybercrime legislation is inherently repressive. Many such laws serve legitimate purposes and reflect genuine efforts to protect citizens’ data. The point is that CMI must exercise independent judgement, informed by context and evidence, rather than treating legal compliance as an automatic proxy for ethical adequacy. In environments where the line between data protection and data extraction is blurred, the institution’s primary obligation is to the people whose data it holds.

## How to Use This Framework

These principles are intended to serve as a starting point and a shared vocabulary for data management planning at CMI. They do not replace the need for project-specific DMPs, which should translate these principles into concrete, operational decisions appropriate to the research at hand.

When developing a DMP, project teams are encouraged to:

1. **Reference these principles explicitly**, noting how each is interpreted and applied in the project context.
1. **Explain departures or adaptations**, where the specifics of the research call for a different emphasis or approach.
1. **Conduct a legal environment assessment** for projects operating in jurisdictions where data protection, cybercrime, or digital governance laws may pose risks to participants (see Principle 13).
1. **Revisit the principles periodically**, particularly when circumstances change or new risks emerge.
1. **Use the principles as a basis for discussion** with partners, funders, and ethics reviewers, to ensure shared understanding and expectations.
