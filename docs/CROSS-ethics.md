# Informed consent and information letters

*Every CMI research project involving human participants requires informed, voluntary consent. The information letter is the primary instrument for achieving this: it informs participants about the research and documents their agreement to take part. This page covers what to include in an information letter, how to adapt the process for fieldwork contexts, and how to handle translations and withdrawal. For the underlying legal framework, see [GDPR and legal compliance](CROSS-gdpr-and-legal-compliance.md).*

## The information letter

The information letter fulfils two functions at once. First, it satisfies GDPR transparency obligations under Articles 13 and 14 by telling participants how their data will be processed. Second, it documents ethical consent: by receiving the information and choosing to take part, participants confirm their voluntary, informed engagement with the research.

When the recommended lawful basis is public interest (Article 6(1)(e)), the information letter **informs**; it does not ask for GDPR consent to process data. Participants are free to decline to participate (their ethical right), but their participation is not contingent on signing a GDPR consent form. For the full rationale on this distinction, see [the consent distinction](CROSS-gdpr-and-legal-compliance.md#the-consent-distinction-ethical-consent-and-gdpr-consent).

## What to include

Every information letter for a project involving human participants should cover the following:

- Who is responsible for the research (CMI and the named researcher)
- What the research is about and why personal data is being processed
- What data will be collected and how
- The lawful basis for processing: public interest (Article 6(1)(e)), with reference to the Norwegian Personal Data Act §8
- For special category data: the additional basis under Article 9(2)(j)
- How data will be stored and secured
- How long data will be retained
- Who will have access to the data (project team, named partner institutions if applicable)
- Participant rights (access, rectification, complaint to Datatilsynet)
- That participation is voluntary and the participant can withdraw at any time
- Contact details for the researcher and the RDM contact (rdm@cmi.no)
- Sikt's contact details as data protection services provider

## Topics to address where relevant

Certain activities should be explicitly described in the information letter so that participants are genuinely informed before choosing to take part. These do not require separate consent forms or checkboxes; participation itself constitutes consent when the participant has been properly informed.

- **Audio or video recording.** Recording is a distinct processing activity with its own implications (voice is arguably biometric data; recordings are inherently identifiable). If the research involves recording, describe this clearly so participants can make an informed choice.
- **Use of real names or identifiable information in publications.** The default is anonymisation. If identifiable information may be used, explain this so participants are aware.
- **Archiving data for future reuse.** If data will be deposited in a repository for secondary use beyond the original project, participants need to be informed so their engagement extends to that purpose.
- **Specific sensitive processing.** Any processing that goes beyond the core research activity, for example sharing identifiable data with a named third party for a defined purpose.

!!! tip "Draft early, revise as needed"
    Draft the information letter during project setup, before data collection begins. Submit it to Sikt with the notification form for review. The letter can be revised if the project scope changes, but participants should always receive current information before they take part.

## Withdrawal deadlines

The information letter should clearly explain the limits on withdrawal. Specifically, it should state that withdrawal is possible up to a defined point (for example, 'until the data have been de-identified' or 'until [date]'), and that after that point, deletion may no longer be feasible. Each project should define a **concrete deadline** in its DMP and information letter. Forthcoming guidance on data retention will cover the practical limits of withdrawal and objection in more detail.

## Oral information delivery

In many CMI fieldwork contexts, standard written information procedures are inappropriate or insufficient. Literacy, language, cultural context, or security considerations may all play a role.

When written delivery is not suitable, deliver the information letter content orally in a language the participant understands. Document that the information was provided through one of the following:

- A note in the research log recording the date, participant (by pseudonym if names are not being recorded), and confirmation that information was delivered
- An audio-recorded verbal confirmation from the participant (if recording is part of the study)
- A witness confirmation (a colleague or research assistant confirms the information was provided)

Written consent elements (for recording, use of real names) can be replaced with oral consent documented in the same way.

!!! warning "When documentation itself creates risk"
    In conflict zones, authoritarian settings, or situations where a signed form linking a participant to research could endanger them, do not produce written consent documentation. Oral information with no written record of the participant's identity may be the most ethical and GDPR-compliant approach. The flexibility described here applies to the method of delivery, not to the substance: participants must always be informed, regardless of the format.

??? example "Planning oral delivery for fieldwork"
    Before fieldwork, prepare an oral information delivery script covering all the standard content listed above. Decide on the documentation method you will use and note this in the DMP and Sikt notification. For high-risk contexts where even oral documentation might create risk, consult the RDM contact (rdm@cmi.no) before departing.

## Translated information letters

When participants do not speak Norwegian or English, or when a local language version would improve comprehension, translate the information letter into the relevant language(s). The translation does not need to be a formal legal document; it needs to be clear and comprehensible. Prioritise clarity over legal precision.

Budget for translation in the project proposal. Have the translation reviewed by a local collaborator or research assistant for accuracy and comprehensibility. Retain both the original and translated versions for your records.

## Consent is not a checkbox

Consent in research is not a bureaucratic exercise. A well-drafted information letter, delivered in a way the participant can understand, with genuine opportunity to ask questions and to decline, is what makes consent meaningful. The goal is not a signed form; it is an informed person who has freely chosen to take part in your research.
