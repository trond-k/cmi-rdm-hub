# Decide whether your project uses personal data

*Personal data is any information that can identify a person, directly or through context. The definition is broader than most researchers expect, and the boundary between personal and anonymous data is often blurrier than it appears. This page explains what counts, where CMI projects commonly misjudge, and why the distinction matters for your project design.*

## The definition

Personal data is any information relating to an identified or identifiable natural person. The key word is *identifiable*. A person is identifiable if they can be singled out, directly or indirectly, by anyone with access to the data and reasonably available means.

The test is not whether you intend to identify anyone. It is whether identification is *possible*. This applies regardless of the format (audio, text, numbers, images), the source (interviews, surveys, registers, social media), or whether the data was collected by you or obtained from others.

## Direct identifiers

Some data elements identify a person on their own: names, national identity numbers, email addresses, telephone numbers, photographs where someone is recognisable, voice recordings. If your project records any of these, even temporarily, it involves personal data.

Voice recordings deserve particular attention. A voice on an audio recording is personal data even if you never ask the participant's name, because the voice itself can identify the speaker. The same applies to video where a person is recognisable. Audio-recorded interviews are always personal data.

## Indirect identification

This is where CMI projects most often misjudge. Data that contains no names or direct identifiers can still be personal data if combinations of variables could single someone out:

- In a small community, age, gender, village, and occupation together may identify an individual.
- A district-level health worker described by role, district, and years of service may be the only person fitting that description.
- GPS coordinates from a household survey in a sparsely populated area can locate a specific homestead.
- A detailed account of events in a qualitative interview may point to one person, even without a name.

The question is always whether someone, using this data together with other reasonably available information, could identify a specific person. In small study populations, the answer is more often yes than researchers expect.

## Pseudonymised data is still personal data

If participants are given codes or pseudonyms but a separate key links those codes back to real identities, the data is personal data. This is true regardless of who holds the key, where it is stored, or how restricted access is. Pseudonymisation reduces risk, but it does not change the legal classification.

The corollary: data is only truly anonymous if re-identification is no longer possible by any reasonable means. Removing names from interview transcripts is not enough if the content and context could still point to a specific person. True anonymisation is harder than it appears, particularly for qualitative data from small populations.

!!! warning "Collecting anonymously is different from anonymising afterwards"
    If you collect personal data and anonymise it later, the collection itself is still processing personal data and requires a Sikt notification. Anonymisation is something you do *to* personal data; it does not retroactively make the collection anonymous. If you want anonymous data, the entire collection process must be designed that way from the start.

## Survey platforms and digital traces

Most online survey tools log respondent email addresses or IP addresses by default. If a platform links responses to identifying information at any point, even temporarily, the responses are personal data. This is true even if the survey questions themselves contain nothing identifying.

A genuinely anonymous survey requires a platform configured to never record respondent identity or network address. If you are unsure whether your platform does this, assume it does until you verify otherwise.

## Third parties in your data

Personal data is not limited to your direct participants. If an interviewee names a local official, describes a colleague's behaviour, or discusses a family member's health, those third parties may become identifiable in the data. When they do, they are data subjects under GDPR, even though they never participated in the research.

This is common in qualitative research at CMI. Interviews about governance, service delivery, or community dynamics routinely produce identifiable references to people who are not present. You cannot seek consent from third parties you did not anticipate. What you can do is recognise that this material is personal data and handle it with the same care.

## Social media and publicly available data

Publicly posted content is personal data if it is linked to an identifiable person. A username, a profile photograph, a searchable quote: these all connect data to an individual. The fact that content was posted publicly does not exempt it from data protection requirements. Research involving social media scraping, online ethnography, or analysis of public discourse typically involves personal data.

## Special categories

Some personal data is treated as particularly sensitive under GDPR: racial or ethnic origin, political opinions, religious or philosophical beliefs, trade union membership, genetic data, biometric data, health data, and data about sex life or sexual orientation.

In CMI's research profile, special categories arise frequently and often incidentally. An interview about governance may capture political opinions. Ethnographic fieldwork in a multi-ethnic context captures ethnic origin. Health research captures health data. If your project involves personal data, consider whether any of it falls into these categories, because special categories require an additional legal basis for processing. See [GDPR and legal compliance](CROSS-gdpr-and-legal-compliance.md) for how this works in practice.

## Making the choice

The personal data question is not a bureaucratic hurdle. It is a design decision with real consequences for methodology, data quality, and administrative effort.

Collecting personal data gives you richer material. Audio recordings preserve nuance that notes cannot. Follow-up interviews, data linkage, and quality control all require identifiers. De-identified data can be archived for reuse in ways that anonymous data sometimes cannot. The cost is a Sikt notification, a legal basis for processing (public interest is the recommended default at CMI), an information letter for participants, and a plan for anonymisation or deletion at the end of the project.

Avoiding personal data is simpler to manage but constrains your methods. You cannot follow up with participants, link to other datasets, or check for duplicates.

Data minimisation does not mean avoiding personal data. It means being deliberate about what you collect and why. If your research questions require identifiable data, collect it with appropriate safeguards. If they do not, design for anonymity from the start rather than retrofitting it later.

!!! tip "If you are unsure"
    Err on the side of assuming your project involves personal data. Sikt's advisers assess over ten thousand projects a year and can tell you quickly whether your design crosses the threshold.
