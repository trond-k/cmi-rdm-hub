### Cross-cutting themes

The following themes are not confined to a single stage but surface throughout the lifecycle. Each stage should address them where relevant.

> **🔒 Sensitive data** — Data sensitivity is a thread that runs from initial screening (Stage 1) through ethics strategy (Stage 2), legal planning (Stage 3), ethical conduct in the field (Stage 4), secure storage (Stage 5), pseudonymisation (Stage 6), dark archiving (Stage 9), discoverability of restricted data (Stage 10), secure access (Stage 11), and ethical reuse (Stage 12). For CMI, operating in conflict-affected and authoritarian contexts, sensitivity is not an edge case — it is the norm.

> **🤖 AI governance** — AI tools appear across collection (Stage 4: automated transcription, NLP), processing (Stage 6: AI-assisted classification), analysis (Stage 7: AI/ML approaches), and reuse (Stage 12: AI training responsibilities). As AI use in research grows, each stage should document the role of AI, including model versions, parameters, training data, and known limitations. Responsible use and transparency are especially important where AI-generated outputs inform findings or policy.

### Foundational perspective: Data, power, and equity

Data management is not a neutral, technical exercise. Decisions about what data is collected, by whom, how it is stored, who controls access, and on whose terms it is shared are shaped by — and reproduce — power relations. For CMI, working across conflict-affected, authoritarian, and development contexts, this is not an abstract concern.

- **Data as representation** — Data amplifies some voices while silencing others. CMI's commitment to equal knowledge production means adopting an explicit role as a voice-amplifier for underrepresented perspectives (see CMI institutional context).
- **Partnership dynamics and equity** — Multi-national collaborations risk centralising data infrastructure in the Global North while Southern partners concentrate on collection. Equitable data governance requires shared ownership, co-authorship, and meaningful influence over how data is used and disseminated.
- **Data sovereignty and community governance** — Communities — particularly marginalised, indigenous, and conflict-affected populations — must retain meaningful influence over how their knowledge is collected, interpreted, and circulated. Frameworks such as CARE (Collective benefit, Authority to control, Responsibility, Ethics) and Ubuntu provide ethical foundations that extend beyond technical compliance.
- **Beyond open/closed binaries** — Responsible data governance involves hybrid models: some materials openly available, others under controlled access, some restricted to metadata-only, and some retained exclusively by local partners. Default openness is not always ethical openness.

This perspective should inform every stage of the lifecycle — from how research questions are framed (Stage 1) to how reuse is governed (Stage 12). It is not a checklist item but a lens through which all data management decisions should be considered.

# Before — Laying the groundwork

*From concept to kick-off: framing the research data landscape, securing resources, and formalising plans before data collection begins.*

These three stages establish the intellectual, administrative, and procedural foundations for data management. The decisions and commitments made here — what data to collect, what to promise funders, how to document plans — set the trajectory for everything that follows. Getting this right reduces friction, cost, and risk throughout the lifecycle.

---

## 1. FRAME (or Design and Conceptualise)
*Distinct focus: intellectual groundwork — what data, why this data, is it feasible, is it justified?*

> **Key principle:** Good data management starts at the concept stage. Decisions made — or not made — here cascade through the entire lifecycle. Early awareness prevents costly redesign later.

- **Matching data to research questions** — What data is actually needed to address the RQ? Distinguishing between "nice to have" and "necessary."
- **Data scope and delimitation** — Setting boundaries early: what will and will not be collected, and why.
- **Landscape review of existing data** — What datasets already exist? Can the RQ be answered (partly) with secondary data? Avoiding unnecessary collection.
- **Feasibility of data collection** — Can the data realistically be obtained? Technical, geographical, financial, and ethical feasibility.
- **Data types and formats (preliminary)** — Qualitative, quantitative, mixed, sensor, text, image, audio, geospatial, administrative, biological — early awareness of what is involved.
- **Data sensitivity screening** — Early identification of personal data, sensitive categories, indigenous/community knowledge, commercial confidentiality.

### Data Inventory status
- **Sketching a base data inventory** — Outline a preliminary data inventory in the concept note; descriptive and awareness-oriented, no need for rigid systematisation at this stage.

### Tool / Template:
- CMI Concept note (standard and extended)

## 2. FUND — Data in Funding Applications

*Distinct focus: external and administrative — aligning the data concept with funder requirements and securing resources for RDM.*

> **Key principle:** The funding application is where RDM commitments become binding. What you promise here — on openness, sharing, and data management — shapes what you must deliver.

- **Data management strategy in the application** — Articulating how data will be managed, stored, and shared; demonstrating awareness and planning without requiring a full DMP at this stage.
- **Ethics strategy in the application** — Addressing personal data, vulnerable populations, dual-use concerns, and commercial sensitivity as part of the ethical framework of the proposal.
- **Funder requirements and data sharing commitments** — Understanding funder mandates for openness, FAIR compliance, Plan S, institutional OA policies, data availability statements, and embargo rules; articulating what the proposal commits to regarding access levels and timelines for sharing.
- **Addressing sensitivities, restrictions, and embargoes** — Justifying any limitations on openness (e.g., participant confidentiality, IP, national security) with clear rationale.
- **Budgeting for RDM** — Costing data management activities: infrastructure (repository fees, secure storage, specialised software licences, transcription, digitisation), personnel (data steward/manager roles, training, capacity-building), tools, curation, and long-term preservation fees.
- **Institutional support and in-kind contributions** — What does the institution provide (storage, repository access, DMP tools, advisory services) and how does this offset grant costs?
- **Multi-funder and international considerations** — Reconciling different funder requirements when co-funded or cross-border.
- **Impact and reuse narratives** — Making the case for data as a research output with reuse value, societal impact, or policy relevance.

### Data Inventory status
- **Refining the data inventory** — Mapping data explicitly to research questions, work packages, and methods; increasing specificity from the design stage. The inventory supports the data management and ethics strategies in the application but is not yet a formal deliverable.

### Tool / Template:
- Snippets and examples: addressing openness and justified restrictions in applications
- Funder requirements overview (RCN, EU/Horizon Europe, ERC, Norad, commissioned work/other funders)
- RDM budget checklist

## 3. PLAN — Data Management Planning

*Distinct focus: procedural documentation — formalising how data will be handled, who is responsible, and what compliance frameworks apply.*

> **Key principle:** The DMP is a living document, not a compliance exercise. It should evolve as the project develops, and be revisited at key milestones (see Monitoring and review below).

- **Writing a Data Management Plan (DMP)** — Formal (funder-facing) and internal/operational versions. Living document vs. static submission. The DMP addresses key topics including: data standards and conventions, participant information and transparency, sensitive and restricted data handling, international data transfers, data retention and destruction, and risk assessment and contingency planning. Tools: DMPonline, DMPTool, institutional templates, funder-specific templates, machine-actionable DMPs.
- **Roles and responsibilities** — Who is responsible for what: PI, data manager, IT, ethics officer, collaborators, students.
- **Collaboration agreements** — Data sharing agreements, consortium agreements, memoranda of understanding, GDPR data processing and controller agreements.
- **Legal and ethics planning** — GDPR and data protection (lawful basis, Sikt notification, incl. DPIA), ethics self-assessment, intellectual property, contractual obligations, data ownership.
- **Preregistration** — Committing to analytical plans before data collection, choosing a registry (OSF, AsPredicted). Well established for quantitative/experimental research; emerging practice for qualitative projects, where registration content focuses on research questions, methodological approach, and analytical framework rather than specific hypotheses and statistical tests.
- **Monitoring and review** — Scheduling regular reviews of the DMP and data inventory at key project milestones (e.g., after data collection, after processing, at reporting deadlines). Checking whether plans still match reality: have new data types emerged? Have storage or access arrangements changed? Are roles and responsibilities still appropriate? Updating the DMP and related documentation accordingly.

### Data Inventory status
- **Formalising the data inventory** — The data inventory becomes a formal, structured document — a key component of and companion to the DMP. It should now specify metadata standards, formats, sensitivity classifications, storage locations, and responsibility assignments for each dataset.

# During — Working with data

*From collection to analysis: gathering, storing, transforming, and interpreting data while keeping it secure, organised, and well-documented.*

These four stages cover the active research phase — where plans meet reality. Data is created or acquired, stored securely, transformed into analysis-ready form, and used to generate findings. The quality of what can be preserved, shared, and reused later depends on the care taken here. Documentation, provenance, and reproducibility are not afterthoughts but integral to the work itself.

---

## 4. COLLECT — Data Gathering

*Distinct focus: empirical engagement — the active creation or acquisition of data, and the practical and ethical conduct of that process.*

> **Key principle:** Data quality is determined at the point of collection. Errors introduced here are the hardest to fix later. Invest in protocols, pilot testing, and real-time quality control.

- **Instruments, protocols, and pilot testing** — Interview guides, survey instruments (household surveys, RCTs), focus group protocols, observation schedules, experimental protocols, sampling frames. Testing instruments, protocols, and workflows before full-scale collection.
- **Ethical conduct and participant information** — Power dynamics, coercion risks, cultural sensitivity, working with vulnerable populations, participant distress protocols. Information obligations, participant rights, and withdrawal procedures.
- **Equipment and tools** — Audio and video recording devices, transcription tools, secure communication platforms, mobile data collection apps (KoBoToolbox, REDCap, ODK), survey platforms.
- **Secondary and external data acquisition** — Accessing register data, administrative records, policy documents, existing datasets, licensed databases, archive materials, web scraping, and APIs. Including legal and ethical considerations, terms of service, data quality, and reproducibility.
- **Field documentation and provenance** — Field notes, ethnographic journals, interview logs, provenance records, contextual metadata captured at the point of collection. Tracking the origin and handling of each data item from the moment of creation.
- **Quality control during collection** — Real-time validation, range checks, skip logic, inter-rater reliability, calibration.
- **Sampling and representativeness** — Sampling strategies, bias awareness, documentation of inclusion/exclusion criteria.
- **Multi-site and multi-modal collection** — Harmonising collection across sites, languages, platforms, and data types.
- **AI-assisted data collection** — Automated transcription, translation tools, NLP-assisted coding — documenting the role of AI in the collection process.

### Data Inventory status
- **Populating the data inventory** — As data is collected, the inventory is updated with actual datasets, collection dates, sources, formats, volumes, and any deviations from what was planned. The inventory becomes a living record of what exists.

### Tool / Template:
- Participant information sheet templates
- Survey, interview and focus groups guides; Field documentation templates (field notes, interview logs)
- Data collection protocol checklist
- Quality control checklist

## 5. STORE — Active Data Storage

*Distinct focus: secure, accessible storage during the project — where data lives while work is ongoing.*

> **Key principle:** Storage decisions during the project directly affect what can be preserved, shared, and reused afterwards. Choose infrastructure that supports the full lifecycle, not just immediate convenience.

- **Storage solutions and capacity** — CMI default: MS365 E5 (OneDrive, SharePoint, Teams) with standard E5 configurations. Special cases requiring additional services: TSD for sensitive personal data, Tresorit or ProtonDrive for end-to-end encrypted sharing. Data residency implications, anticipating growth, large-file handling.
- **Backup strategy** — MS365 built-in retention and versioning as default; when additional backup is needed, verification and testing.
- **Security and sensitive data handling** — MS365 E5 security features (access controls, MFA, endpoint security) as baseline. Secure environments for personal and sensitive data: TSD (Norway), Tresorit, ProtonDrive. Data separation and compartmentalisation: storing identifiers separately from research data, key files, linking strategies.
- **File organisation and version control** — Folder structures, naming conventions, README files, separation of raw/processed/analysis/working data. Git for code and text, dataset versioning, change tracking. Distinguishing scratch files and intermediate outputs from authoritative data.
- **Collaboration and synchronisation** — Shared drives, Git repositories, project management tools — balancing collaboration with security. Multi-site workflows, offline/online synchronisation, conflict resolution in shared environments.
- **Compliance with storage requirements** — Funder mandates, institutional policies, legal obligations (e.g., data must remain within a jurisdiction).

*Data Inventory: update as part of ongoing monitoring (see Stage 3).*

## 6. PROCESS — Data Transformation

*Distinct focus: the bridge between raw collection and analysis-ready data — technical transformation, quality assurance, and documentation of changes.*

> **Key principle:** Every transformation must be documented. Undocumented processing is irreproducible processing. If a step can't be traced, the data can't be trusted.

- **Data cleaning and quality assessment** — Identifying and handling errors, outliers, duplicates, missing values, inconsistencies. Assessing completeness, accuracy, consistency, and timeliness — documented quality metrics.
- **Pseudonymisation and anonymisation** — Techniques (generalisation, suppression, k-anonymity), risk of re-identification, documentation of decisions and methods applied.
- **Transcription and digitisation** — Audio/video to text (manual vs. automated/AI), quality assurance, speaker identification, handling of dialect/language. Scanning physical documents, OCR.
- **Format conversion and data structuring** — Converting proprietary to open formats, ensuring interoperability, preserving fidelity. Tidy data principles, normalisation, relational structuring, long vs. wide formats.
- **Coding and classification** — Qualitative coding (initial, thematic), applying taxonomies, variable recoding, categorisation.
- **Data integration and linkage** — Combining datasets from multiple sources, entity resolution, harmonisation of variables and coding schemes.
- **Reproducible processing and documentation** — Scripted workflows (R, Python, shell scripts), AI-assisted processing (NLP for text data, automated classification — documenting model versions and parameters). Processing logs, transformation records, codebooks, provenance chains. Documenting the relationship between raw and derived datasets — ensuring that any researcher can understand what was done and why.

*Data Inventory: update as part of ongoing monitoring (see Stage 3).*

### Tool / Template:
- Codebook template or guide/resource
- Processing log template
- Pseudonymisation/anonymisation introduction/clarification and decision guide

## 7. ANALYSE — Data Analysis and Interpretation

*Distinct focus: epistemic engagement — using processed data to generate findings, with emphasis on reproducibility and methodological transparency.*

> **Key principle:** Reproducibility starts with the analyst, not the reader. If you can't reproduce your own work, no one else can either. Document decisions, not just results.

- **Analytical methods and tools** — Statistical modelling, qualitative coding (NVivo, Atlas.ti), mixed-methods integration, geospatial analysis, network analysis, AI/ML approaches (documenting model choices and parameters), exploratory visualisation.
- **Qualitative analysis practices** — Thematic analysis, grounded theory, discourse analysis, reflexivity, analytical memos, inter-coder agreement.
- **Reproducibility and analytical documentation** — Code versioning (Git), dependency management (renv, conda), computational notebooks (Jupyter, R Markdown, Quarto), software and environment documentation. Analytical notebooks and decision logs — capturing *why* choices were made, not just *what*. Documenting the lineage of derived datasets (model results, coded data, aggregations).
- **Collaborative analysis** — Multi-researcher analytical workflows, division of labour, reconciliation of interpretations.
- **Validation and sensitivity analysis** — Robustness checks, cross-validation, triangulation, assessing the stability of findings.
- **Research integrity and transparency** — Adhering to preregistered plans (see Stage 3) and documenting deviations; distinguishing confirmatory from exploratory analysis. Documenting negative results and null findings. Avoiding p-hacking, HARKing, selective reporting; responsible use of AI-generated insights.

*Data Inventory: update as part of ongoing monitoring (see Stage 3).*

### Tool / Template:
- Analytical documentation template (methods, software, decisions)
- Reproducibility checklist

# After — Releasing and sustaining

*From publication to long-term stewardship: making outputs available, ensuring they endure, and governing how others find, access, and reuse them.*

These five stages address what happens once the active research phase ends. Data and outputs are formally published, preserved for the long term, made findable, governed for access, and prepared for meaningful reuse. For CMI, where much data is sensitive and contexts are politically complex, this phase requires careful balancing of visibility with protection — and a recognition that trustworthy archives handle much of the technical infrastructure by design.

---

## 8. PUBLISH — Formal Dissemination of Data and Outputs

*Distinct focus: the formal, citable release of research outputs — making data, code, and findings publicly available through recognised channels.*

> **Key principle:** Choosing the right archive is the single most impactful FAIR decision a researcher makes. Trustworthy, certified archives provide persistent identifiers, standardised metadata, access controls, and long-term preservation by design. The researcher's most important contribution is providing rich, accurate metadata and documentation.

- **Publishing research outputs** — Data in archives: Sikt Research Data Archive (default), OSF and Zenodo as alternatives. Articles and other outputs in NVA (Nasjonalt vitenarkiv). Code (GitHub/GitLab with DOI). Data papers (Scientific Data, JOHD), preprints (SocArXiv, OSF Preprints). Preparing and packaging deposits: cleaning, documenting, ensuring code/documentation/workflows are included alongside data. Handling versioning, corrections, and updates to published outputs.
- **Trustworthy archives and FAIR** — Trustworthy, certified archives provide much of the FAIR principles by design (see key principle above). Understanding what archives offer helps researchers focus their effort where it matters most: rich metadata and thorough documentation.
- **Persistent identifiers and data citation** — DOIs for datasets and code, ORCIDs for researchers, ROR IDs for institutions. Following FORCE11 data citation principles — citing data as a first-class research output. Bidirectional linking between articles, datasets, code, and supplementary materials.
- **Publisher requirements and data availability statements** — Journal data policies, embargo periods and funder/publisher rules on timing, writing clear availability statements: where the data is, how to access it, any restrictions.
- **Reproducibility packages and supplementary materials** — Bundling data, code, and documentation for independent verification. What goes in supplements vs. repositories; best practices for appendices, extended data, and multimedia.

### Data Inventory status
- **Finalising the inventory with publication details** — The inventory should now record where each dataset and output has been published, with persistent identifiers, access conditions, and embargo timelines.

## 9. PRESERVE — Long-term Data Preservation

*Distinct focus: post-project stewardship — ensuring data remains intact, interpretable, and accessible long after the active research phase ends.*

> **Key principle:** Trustworthy archives handle much of preservation by design — format migration, integrity checks, and metadata curation. The researcher's role is to choose the right archive (see Stage 8) and provide well-documented, well-structured data.

- **Format sustainability and preservation standards** — Choosing preservation-friendly formats (CSV, PDF/A, ODF), avoiding proprietary lock-in. Planning for migration and format obsolescence.
- **Preservation metadata and integrity** — Metadata for long-term interpretability and discovery (Dublin Core, DataCite, PREMIS). Checksums, fixity checks, periodic audits.
- **Retention and destruction** — Legal minimums (GDPR), funder requirements, institutional policies. Scheduled secure deletion when data must not be retained beyond a specified period — certificates of destruction.
- **Dark archiving** — Preserving sensitive, embargoed, or restricted data that cannot be openly shared but must be retained.
- **Cost of long-term preservation** — Institutional commitment, funder exit strategies, sustainable models for repositories.

### Data Inventory status
- **Closing the inventory loop** — The inventory should now serve as the authoritative record of what has been preserved, where, under what conditions, and for how long — linking back to the full lifecycle from collection through publication.

### Tool / Template:
- Preservation format guide
- Retention and destruction schedule template.

## 10. DISCOVER — Findability

*Distinct focus: making data visible and findable — metadata, identifiers, and indexing that enable others to locate the data.*

> **Key principle:** Trustworthy archives handle much of technical discoverability by design — machine-readable metadata, landing pages, harvesting, and search indexing. The researcher's most impactful contributions are choosing the right archive (see Stage 8) and providing rich, accurate metadata.

- **Metadata standards and vocabularies** — Choosing and applying appropriate metadata schemas (DataCite, Dublin Core, DDI) and controlled vocabularies for subject classification. The researcher's key contribution to findability.
- **Persistent identifiers and cross-referencing** — PIDs assigned at publication (see Stage 8) enable findability through linking datasets to publications, projects, funders, and other datasets. Aligns with FAIR Findability principles (F1–F4).
- **Discoverability of restricted data** — Making metadata openly findable even when the data itself is restricted — "metadata always open." Critical for CMI's sensitive data contexts.
- **Multilingual discovery** — Metadata in multiple languages, supporting cross-linguistic search. Relevant for CMI's multi-language, Global South research contexts.
- **Technical discoverability infrastructure** — Machine-readable metadata, landing pages, harvesting and aggregation, data catalogues, search engine indexing (see key principle above).

### Tool / Template:
- Metadata checklist (what to include for findability)
- Vocabulary selection guide.

## 11. ACCESS — Conditions for Data Retrieval

*Distinct focus: who can get the data, under what terms, and through what mechanisms — the governance of retrieval.*

> **Key principle:** Trustworthy archives handle much of access infrastructure by design — authentication, request workflows, download mechanisms, and audit trails. The researcher's key decisions are choosing the right access model for each dataset and defining clear governance terms.

- **Access levels and tiered models** — Open, embargoed, restricted, closed — defining and justifying the access model for each dataset. Tiered mechanisms: public use files, licensed access, secure access (virtual safe rooms, trusted research environments).
- **Access governance** — Data use agreements specifying terms of use (purpose limitations, redistribution prohibitions, reporting requirements). Access request workflows (application, review, approval). Monitoring and audit trails — tracking who accesses data, when, and for what purpose.
- **Access mechanisms and secure environments** — Authentication, secure desktops (microdata.no, Sikt), trusted research environments (TREs), federated approaches (bringing analysis to data rather than data to researcher, data mesh architectures for decentralised data governance across partners). Largely provided by archives and platforms (see key principle above).
- **Sunsetting and withdrawal** — What happens when data must be withdrawn? Tombstone records, metadata persistence, retraction procedures. Aligns with FAIR Accessibility principle A2 (metadata remains accessible even when data is no longer available).

## 12. SHARE & REUSE — Enabling Downstream Use

*Distinct focus: making data meaningfully usable by others — licensing, documentation, interoperability, and the culture of reuse.*

> **Key principle:** Sharing is not just a technical act — it is a governance decision. For CMI, operating in sensitive and politically complex contexts, meaningful sharing requires balancing openness with protection. CARE principles and equitable partnership norms (see CMI institutional context) guide what responsible sharing looks like.

- **Licensing and licence compatibility** — Creative Commons (CC BY, CC0), Open Data Commons, bespoke licences — choosing terms that enable reuse while reflecting rights and constraints. Compatibility when integrating multiple datasets, attribution stacking, provenance of composite datasets.
- **Documentation for reuse** — Who are the likely reusers (researchers, policymakers, journalists, the public, machines/AI)? Codebooks, data dictionaries, variable descriptions, methodology guides, README files. Documenting known limitations, biases, coverage gaps. Meeting domain-relevant community standards (FAIR Principle R).
- **Interoperability** — Standard formats, vocabularies, linked data — FAIR Principle I (data uses formal, accessible, shared, and broadly applicable language).
- **Ethical reuse and data sovereignty** — Preventing harmful reuse (surveillance, discrimination, re-identification), participant expectations. AI training responsibilities (bias, representativeness, licence compatibility). CARE principles (Collective benefit, Authority to control, Responsibility, Ethics) — ensuring sharing respects sovereignty of communities and marginalised populations. Emerging models: data trusts and cooperatives.
- **Tracking reuse and impact** — Data citations, download counts, altmetrics. Feedback mechanisms for reusers to report errors, contribute improvements, or share derived outputs.

- Reuse documentation checklist (what a stranger needs to understand your data)
- CARE principles quick reference

## Note on possible simplification

The 12 stages above provide granular coverage of the full data lifecycle. For some purposes — particularly navigation and onboarding — a simpler structure may be more effective. The stages group naturally into three phases:

| Phase | Stages |
|---|---|
| **Before** (pre-data) | 1. FRAME → 2. FUND → 3. PLAN |
| **During** (active research) | 4. COLLECT → 5. STORE → 6. PROCESS → 7. ANALYSE |
| **After** (post-project) | 8. PUBLISH → 9. PRESERVE → 10. DISCOVER → 11. ACCESS → 12. SHARE & REUSE |

The **After** phase (5 stages) is the most amenable to consolidation. Two potential merges:

- **PUBLISH + DISCOVER** — Most of DISCOVER is either handled by the archive chosen at PUBLISH or is the outcome of good publishing practices (rich metadata, PIDs). The independently actionable researcher content in DISCOVER is thin.
- **ACCESS + SHARE & REUSE** — Both deal with "how others use your data." ACCESS covers mechanisms and governance of retrieval; SHARE & REUSE covers terms, documentation, and culture of reuse. These could combine into a single stage on enabling and governing downstream use.

This would give a **10-stage model**: 3 Before + 4 During + 3 After (PUBLISH & DISCOVER → PRESERVE → SHARE & GOVERN). The full 12-stage structure would remain as the detailed reference underneath.
