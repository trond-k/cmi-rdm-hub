# Open Science
*Trond Kvamme*

## What is Open Science?

Open Science is both a broad philosophy and a set of practices aimed at making research as transparent, accessible, and collaborative as possible. It involves sharing knowledge, results, and tools early and widely, promoting reproducibility and accessibility across all disciplines. Open Science seeks to remove barriers to knowledge, ensuring that scientific research can be accessed and used by researchers, policymakers, and the wider public.

According to a UNESCO report, Open Science is:

> "[...] an inclusive construct that combines various movements and practices aiming to make multilingual scientific knowledge openly available, accessible and reusable for everyone, to increase scientific collaborations and sharing of information for the benefits of science and society, and to open the processes of scientific knowledge creation, evaluation and communication to societal actors beyond the traditional scientific community." [^1]

Open Science is relevant across all academic fields, including the natural and social sciences, and is structured around four key pillars:

**Pillar 1: Open Scientific Knowledge:** The foundation of Open Science, ensuring that scientific outputs are freely available for access, reuse, and redistribution. This includes:

- **Open Research Publications (often referred to as Open Access):** Peer-reviewed articles, books, reports, and conference papers that are freely accessible, often through institutional repositories or open-access journals.

- **Open Research Data:** Datasets that are made publicly available following principles such as FAIR (Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, and Reusable), allowing others to validate, replicate, or build upon existing research.

- **Open Methodologies:** Transparency in research design, data collection, and analysis processes, enabling scrutiny and reproducibility of scientific findings.

- **Open Educational Resources (OER):** Teaching and research materials that can be freely used, adapted, and shared to support education and training.

- **Open Source Software and Hardware:** Research software and tools, including algorithms, models, and hardware designs, that are openly licensed to facilitate further innovation and collaboration.

**Pillar 2: Open Science Infrastructures:** Platforms and repositories that support the storage, sharing, and preservation of open research outputs. These infrastructures include open-access journals, data repositories, and digital tools that enable collaboration.

**Pillar 3: Open Engagement of Societal Actors:** Encouraging collaboration between researchers and non-academic stakeholders, such as policymakers, industry representatives, and the public. This includes participatory research methods, citizen science initiatives, and knowledge co-production efforts.

**Pillar 4: Open Dialogue with Other Knowledge Systems:** Recognising and integrating diverse knowledge traditions, including indigenous and local knowledge, into scientific research. This encourages inclusivity and respects different epistemological perspectives.

## Open Science, Open Access, and Open Data

As discussed above, Open Science encompasses a range of practices designed to enhance the accessibility and transparency of research. In research, two key components of Open Science are Open Access and Open Data, which are sometimes used interchangeably but have distinct meanings:

**Open Access (OA)** refers to making scientific publications freely available online without financial, legal, or technical barriers[^3]. This ensures that research outputs can be read and reused without subscription fees. Open Access is typically achieved through:

- **Gold Open Access:** Articles are made freely available immediately upon publication, often under a Creative Commons license. Publishing costs are typically covered by Article Processing Charges (APCs).

- **Green Open Access:** Authors self-archive a version of their manuscript in an open repository, making it accessible after any embargo period imposed by the publisher.

**Open Data** involves making research data freely available for reuse. To ensure data is useful, it should follow the FAIR principles [^2]:

- **Findable:** Easily discoverable through indexing and metadata.
- **Accessible:** Available under clear conditions.
- **Interoperable:** Compatible with different datasets and tools.
- **Reusable:** Well-documented and structured to support further research.

While Open Access and Open Data are both central to Open Science, they serve different but complementary functions. Open Access broadens the reach of scholarly publications, while Open Data facilitates the sharing and reuse of underlying research materials. Together, they support the broader goal of making scientific knowledge widely accessible and more participatory.

## Challenges of Open Science in the social sciences

While Open Science has roots in early academic and scholarly traditions of intellectual exchange, its modern principles — particularly those related to Open Data — have been shaped largely by the natural sciences. Large-scale collaborations in fields such as physics, genomics, and environmental science (e.g., CERN, the Human Genome Project, and climate science initiatives) have relied on data sharing and transparency, leading to the development of open repositories such as GenBank (for genetic sequences) and PANGAEA (for earth sciences).

However, applying Open Data principles in the social sciences presents distinct challenges that require careful consideration:

- **Complexity of data** -- Social science datasets often include unstructured or qualitative materials such as interview transcripts, ethnographic field notes, archival documents, and multimedia sources. Unlike structured numerical data, qualitative data is typically context-dependent, making standardization and reuse more complex.

- **Ethical and legal considerations** -- Protecting research participants is a primary concern in the social sciences. While structured datasets can often be anonymized, qualitative data frequently contains contextual details that may inadvertently reveal identities. For example, an interview transcript may disclose a participant's identity through personal narratives or references to specific events.

- **Fieldwork and sensitive data** -- Some social science research involves politically sensitive topics, vulnerable communities, or indigenous knowledge. In such cases, openly sharing data may conflict with ethical obligations to protect participants, maintain confidentiality, and uphold trust within communities.

- **Researcher-participant relationships** -- Social science research often involves ongoing, dynamic relationships between researchers and participants, particularly in ethnographic and anthropological studies. In such contexts, consent is not always a one-time event but an evolving process shaped by trust, reciprocity, and long-term engagement. This flexibility may be difficult to reconcile with Open Data mandates that require predefined data-sharing plans, which assume static consent and clearly delineated data ownership.

## Balancing openness with ethical responsibilities

Adopting flexible and ethical data-sharing models means implementing strategies that enable openness while respecting security, privacy, consent, and community trust. Not all data can — or should — be openly shared without restrictions. Ethical data-sharing models recognise these limitations and provide alternative approaches, such as:

### Tiered or controlled access

Instead of full public release, researchers can use controlled access repositories or other mechanisms to regulate data availability under specific conditions. Access may require justification of the intended purpose, along with approval from the principal investigator (PI) or a project-specific data access committee. Researchers may also need to sign a data-sharing agreement or data use agreement specifying conditions for responsible use, such as prohibiting re-identification efforts or restricting data redistribution.

**Example:** Some repositories, such as Zenodo and OSF, allow restricted access to sensitive datasets, ensuring ethical oversight. Another alternative is sharing data through a project- or institutionally controlled website. In such cases, a data access policy could outline who can request access, under what conditions, and with what commitments (e.g., signing an agreement and/or undergoing an ethics review).

### Sharing metadata instead of full data

When full data sharing is not feasible due to privacy concerns, legal restrictions, or ethical commitments, sharing metadata can be a useful alternative. Metadata provides structured information about a dataset — such as its scope, methodology, and access conditions — without exposing sensitive content. This allows other researchers to identify potentially relevant datasets and request access where appropriate.

For those sharing metadata, this approach increases the visibility of their research, facilitates collaboration, and enables citation and recognition, even when the underlying data cannot be openly shared.

**Example:** An anthropologist conducting research with Indigenous communities records oral histories containing culturally sensitive information. Instead of sharing the full recordings, they create metadata describing the research — such as the themes covered, data collection methods, and conditions for access (e.g., requiring community approval or a formal request). This allows other researchers to learn about the existence of the dataset and potentially seek ethical access while ensuring the recordings remain protected.

### Anonymisation, de-identification, and selective retention

When possible, researchers can remove or mask identifying details before sharing data. However, for qualitative and ethnographic research, complete anonymisation may not be possible without distorting meaning or erasing important contextual information. In such cases, alternative approaches can help balance ethical considerations with the need to preserve the richness of the data:

- **Aggregating data to reduce identifiability** -- For studies involving multiple participants, researchers can summarise themes or patterns rather than presenting individual narratives, reducing the risk of identification.

- **Removing direct identifiers while maintaining contextual integrity** -- Names, locations, and other directly identifying details can be removed or altered, while preserving enough context for meaningful analysis.

- **Using pseudonyms or data generalisation methods** -- Instead of removing all identifying details, researchers may replace them with pseudonyms, vague descriptors (e.g., "a senior government official" instead of a named minister), or broader categories (e.g., "a town in northern Eritrea" rather than a specific village).

- **Selectively retaining certain data formats while deleting others** -- Some data formats carry higher risks of re-identification. For instance, researchers may delete audio or video recordings while keeping anonymised transcripts or redact sensitive passages while preserving general insights.

**Example:** In a study on political activism, a researcher may remove the names of interviewees and specific locations but retain anonymised quotes that capture key arguments. If recordings pose a risk of voice recognition, they may be deleted while anonymised transcripts are shared under restricted access.

### Ethical informed consent for data sharing

Consent processes should explicitly address data sharing, ensuring participants understand how their data may be used in the future. Ethical models include:

- **Tiered consent:** Participants choose levels of data sharing (e.g., fully open, restricted to academic use, or no sharing).

- **Ongoing consent:** Particularly in long-term ethnographic research, consent is revisited as relationships evolve.

- **Community consent:** For research involving Indigenous or marginalized groups, broader community approvals may be necessary.

**Example:** In ethnographic fieldwork, researchers may renegotiate consent with participants and communities over time rather than securing a one-time agreement.

### Embargo periods

In some cases, immediate data sharing may not be appropriate due to ethical, political, or security concerns. Embargo periods allow researchers to delay data release for a set time to minimize risks to participants, researchers, or affected communities.

Embargoes may be necessary when:

- **Research involves politically sensitive topics** -- Publicly releasing data too soon may endanger participants, particularly in authoritarian regimes, conflict settings, or post-conflict societies.

- **Longitudinal studies are ongoing** -- Making partial datasets available could compromise data integrity or affect participant trust in later phases.

- **Communities need time for review and consent** -- Some Indigenous and marginalized communities may require additional consultation before agreeing to data sharing.

- **Publication or policy considerations apply** -- Some researchers delay data release to align with academic publications or to prevent premature policy conclusions based on incomplete findings.

**Example 1:** A study on post-conflict reconciliation may impose a five-year embargo on interview data to ensure that political tensions have eased before transcripts become available. Even after the embargo ends, access may remain restricted to approved researchers.

**Example 2:** A longitudinal study on migration patterns might delay releasing personal narratives until the study concludes, preventing participants from being identified or targeted while they are still in transit.

### Ethical data repository selection

Choosing a repository that supports controlled access, metadata richness (and metadata only), licensing options, compliance with GDPR and Norwegian data protection laws, and offers responsible stewardship ensures ethical commitments are upheld.

Repositories relevant to CMI researchers:

- **Zenodo** — A multidisciplinary repository developed by OpenAIRE and CERN, widely used for sharing publications and datasets. Supports open access, embargo, and restricted access options. Free for all researchers. CMI has an institutional community for branded deposit. Hosts a dedicated community for Horizon Europe and earlier Framework Programme outputs.

- **OSF (Open Science Framework)** — Supports both open and restricted data sharing, with flexible licensing and metadata options. Particularly useful for replication packages that combine data, code, and manuscripts in a single project, as well as pre-registrations and collaborative research workflows. Free for all researchers.

- **openICPSR** — A self-publishing repository for social science data, open to all researchers worldwide at no cost. Strong heritage in quantitative social science data, including international and comparative datasets.

- **QDR (Qualitative Data Repository)** — The only major repository purpose-built for qualitative and multi-method social science data. Provides expert curation including disclosure risk evaluation, and supports mediated and restricted access — making it particularly relevant for sensitive interview transcripts, field notes, and ethnographic material from CMI's research contexts. Per-deposit fees apply ($500–1,500; fee waivers may be available).

- **Sikt — Research Data Archive** — Part of CESSDA, primarily designed for quantitative survey and register data. Provides mediated access mechanisms. Free for state-funded institutions. **Important limitation:** Sikt does not accept anonymised qualitative data — they require documented participant consent for archiving and future reuse. This means Sikt is suitable for survey datasets and for new qualitative projects where archiving consent is built into the research design from the start, but not for most existing qualitative data collections where consent covered only the original study.

## Why this matters

Flexible and ethical data-sharing models enable researchers to engage with Open Science while maintaining ethical integrity. By integrating controlled access, metadata-sharing, anonymisation, and informed consent strategies, researchers can contribute to knowledge exchange without compromising participant rights, contextual integrity, or ethical obligations.
