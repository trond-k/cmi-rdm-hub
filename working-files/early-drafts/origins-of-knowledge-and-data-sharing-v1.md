# Origins of knowledge and data sharing

The impulse to share what you know is not a modern invention. Long before research data management became a professional discipline, scholars built networks, institutions, and norms around the idea that knowledge grows through exchange. Understanding where these practices came from helps you see today's data-sharing expectations not as bureaucratic impositions but as the latest expression of a very old commitment.

## Scholarship as correspondence

For most of recorded history, knowledge circulated through personal networks. Ancient libraries at Alexandria, Nalanda, and Timbuktu served as nodes where texts were copied, translated, and debated. Medieval Islamic scholars developed the *isnad*, a chain-of-transmission system for verifying the provenance of reported sayings, which arguably represents one of the earliest formalised approaches to documenting data lineage.

In early modern Europe, the 'Republic of Letters' connected natural philosophers, physicians, and mathematicians through a web of private correspondence. Figures like Marin Mersenne and Henry Oldenburg acted as brokers, circulating findings across borders and languages. When the Royal Society of London began publishing *Philosophical Transactions* in 1665, it was formalising what letter-writers had been doing for decades: making observations available for scrutiny by others.

!!! info "Sharing was never universal"
    These early networks were exclusive. Participation depended on social standing, institutional affiliation, language, and geography. The history of knowledge sharing is also a history of knowledge gatekeeping, and that tension has not disappeared.

## From journals to datasets

The scientific journal solved one problem (how to disseminate findings) while creating another (how to verify them). For centuries, the published article was treated as a self-contained record. The underlying data, whether astronomical observations, botanical specimens, or census tabulations, remained with the researcher or the institution that funded the work.

This began to change in the twentieth century, driven by three forces:

1. **Large-scale collaboration.** Projects in physics, meteorology, and genomics generated datasets too large and too expensive for any single group to reproduce. Sharing became a practical necessity.
2. **Computing.** Digital data could be copied without degradation and transmitted without physical transport. The technical barrier to sharing dropped dramatically from the 1960s onwards.
3. **Public accountability.** As public funding of research grew, so did the argument that publicly funded data should be publicly accessible. The Bermuda Principles of 1996, which required immediate release of human genome sequence data, marked a turning point.

By the early 2000s, funders, publishers, and research institutions were beginning to treat data as a first-class research output rather than a private by-product of the research process.

## The open science movement

The term 'open science' gained traction in the 2010s, but the underlying ideas are older. The Budapest Open Access Initiative (2002) and the Berlin Declaration on Open Access to Knowledge in the Sciences and Humanities (2003) established the principle that publicly funded research outputs should be freely available. The FAIR principles, published in 2016, shifted the conversation from simple availability to structured usability: data should be Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, and Reusable.[^1]

These frameworks did not emerge in a vacuum. They responded to real problems that researchers were already encountering: irreproducible results, duplicated effort, and datasets locked behind paywalls or buried on retired hard drives. Open science is, at its core, an argument that research is more reliable and more efficient when its building blocks are visible.

??? example "Open science in practice at research institutes"
    For an institute like CMI, which works across disciplines and geographies, open science raises specific questions. How do you share qualitative interview data without compromising participant confidentiality? How do you respect the data sovereignty of communities in the Global South while meeting European funder mandates? These are not abstract dilemmas; they shape day-to-day decisions about what to deposit, where, and under what conditions.

## Power, provenance, and the politics of sharing

Any honest account of knowledge sharing must reckon with its politics. Colonial-era natural history collections were built through extraction: specimens, vocabularies, and cultural artefacts taken from colonised peoples and deposited in European institutions, often without consent or attribution. The legacy of these practices shapes contemporary debates about who controls research data and who benefits from its reuse.

The CARE Principles for Indigenous Data Governance (Collective Benefit, Authority to Control, Responsibility, Ethics) emerged in 2019 as a direct response to this history. Where FAIR focuses on making data technically usable, CARE asks who has the right to govern it. For researchers working in cross-cultural or post-colonial contexts, these two frameworks are complementary, not competing.

!!! warning "Openness is not always ethical"
    Data sharing is the default aspiration in most funder policies, but it is not an unconditional good. Sharing sensitive data without adequate safeguards can cause harm. Sharing data extracted from vulnerable communities without their involvement in governance can reproduce colonial dynamics. The question is not simply 'should you share?' but 'how, with whom, and on whose terms?'

## Why this history matters for your work

You do not need to become a historian of science to manage your data well. But knowing that data sharing has always been shaped by technology, power, and institutional norms can help you navigate the present landscape with more confidence. When a funder asks for a Data Management Plan (DMP), they are drawing on decades of policy development. When a repository asks you to assign a Digital Object Identifier (DOI) and structured metadata, they are extending practices that date back to library cataloguing and beyond.

The tools and expectations will continue to evolve. What remains constant is the underlying logic: research that others can examine, reuse, and build upon is research that earns trust.
