# Philosophical and epistemic foundations of data sharing

*Data sharing rests on epistemic commitments, not just funder mandates. Merton's communalism norm treats knowledge as a collective inheritance. The FAIR principles operationalise the ideal of reproducibility. But openness is not unconditional: critical perspectives from Leonelli and the CARE Principles show that responsible sharing must also account for epistemic diversity, power, and the rights of the communities from whom data originate.*

## Knowledge as a collective enterprise

The most influential epistemic argument for data sharing traces back to the sociologist Robert K. Merton, who in 1942 described four norms he considered essential to the functioning of science: communalism, universalism, disinterestedness, and organised scepticism.[^1] The first of these, communalism (originally 'communism'), holds that scientific findings are the product of collective effort and therefore constitute a common heritage. No individual researcher's claim to ownership can override the community's need for access. As Merton put it, scientific advance 'involves the collaboration of past and present generations', and its results must be shared if future work is to build on them.[^2]

This norm provides the philosophical backbone for contemporary open science policy. When funders require you to deposit your data in a repository, they are operationalising a Mertonian principle: the research was publicly funded, the knowledge it produces belongs to the commons, and withholding it without justification undermines the epistemic machinery of science itself.

## Reproducibility and the self-correcting ideal

A second epistemic argument concerns the reliability of scientific claims. Organised scepticism, another of Merton's norms, demands that findings be open to scrutiny. Data sharing is a precondition for this. If your results cannot be independently verified because the underlying data are unavailable, the community's capacity for self-correction is diminished. The FAIR principles (Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable), articulated by Wilkinson and colleagues in 2016, formalise this insight into a practical framework: data should be structured and described in ways that make verification and reuse possible, not just in principle but in practice.[^3]

Yet reproducibility is not a neutral technical standard. It privileges certain kinds of evidence (quantitative, structured, computationally tractable) and certain modes of inquiry. Qualitative researchers, ethnographers, and scholars working with sensitive populations often cannot share their data without violating the trust on which the research depends. The epistemic ideal of openness must therefore be balanced against the epistemic value of the relationships that made the research possible in the first place.

## Openness as connection, not extraction

Sabina Leonelli's recent philosophical work on open science challenges the dominant 'object-oriented' framing, in which openness is equated with making research outputs freely available.[^4] She argues that this interpretation risks constraining epistemic diversity and worsening epistemic injustice. When openness means depositing datasets in formats and infrastructures designed by and for well-resourced institutions, researchers working in different contexts (with different languages, norms, and constraints) may find their knowledge devalued or misappropriated.

Leonelli proposes an alternative: openness as 'judicious connection', grounded in a process-oriented epistemology that recognises research as situated, embodied, and goal-directed. On this view, sharing data responsibly means attending not just to access but to the conditions under which data were produced and the relationships that sustain their meaning. This resonates strongly with the realities of research at an institute like CMI, where fieldwork often depends on trust built over years with communities that have their own legitimate interests in how their stories are told.

## Data sovereignty and the limits of universalism

The CARE Principles for Indigenous Data Governance (Collective Benefit, Authority to Control, Responsibility, Ethics) represent a direct challenge to the assumption that data sharing is always and everywhere a good thing.[^5] Developed by the Global Indigenous Data Alliance, CARE foregrounds the rights of Indigenous peoples to govern data that originate from or describe their communities, knowledges, and territories. Where the FAIR principles are data-centric and primarily concerned with reusability, CARE is people-centric and concerned with power.

This is not a niche concern. Any researcher working with marginalised or vulnerable communities faces a version of the same tension: the epistemic goods of openness (transparency, verifiability, cumulative knowledge) can conflict with the ethical goods of self-determination, privacy, and protection from harm. Responsible data sharing requires you to hold both sets of values simultaneously, not to resolve the tension prematurely in favour of one side.

## CARE beyond Indigenous contexts: conflict and humanitarian crises

The logic of CARE extends well beyond Indigenous data governance. Suchikova and Nazarovets have recently argued that the same principles apply to any population whose data can be weaponised against them, including internally displaced persons, civilians living under military occupation, and communities caught in humanitarian crises.[^6]

Their argument is concrete. In occupied territories, digital records that seem routine in peacetime (university enrolment lists, faculty profiles, class schedules) become tools for identifying and targeting individuals deemed politically undesirable. Biometric databases assembled for humanitarian aid can be repurposed for surveillance. International humanitarian law offers no explicit protections for electronic data equivalent to those afforded to physical objects, a gap that matters increasingly as conflict becomes digitised.

The paper proposes adapting each CARE principle to conflict-affected settings: data should serve the collective benefit of affected communities (not just the organisations collecting it); those communities should retain authority to control how their information is used; institutions handling conflict data bear a heightened responsibility to build trust through transparency; and the ethical obligation to prevent harm takes precedence over the epistemic goods of openness.

This matters directly for research at CMI. If you work in or on conflict-affected regions, the data you collect about displacement, governance, livelihoods, or political violence may put your participants at risk in ways that standard anonymisation cannot fully address. The CARE framework, extended in this way, offers a principled basis for restricting access, mandating data destruction after a project ends, or withholding data entirely when the risks of sharing outweigh the benefits.

## What this means for your practice

These philosophical foundations are not abstract. They shape the practical decisions you make every day: whether to deposit data openly or under restricted access; how to describe your data so others can interpret them; whether to anonymise, pseudonymise, or withhold; and how to negotiate data governance with your collaborators and participants. The aspiration to share is grounded in sound epistemic reasoning. So are the reasons to be careful about how, when, and with whom you share.
