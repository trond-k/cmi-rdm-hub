# Core principles for research data at CMI

These principles guide research data management across the full project lifecycle at CMI. They are intended to support judgement in practice, not replace it. Different projects involve different data, risks, obligations, and possibilities for sharing. The purpose of these principles is to provide a common institutional frame for making data-related decisions in a consistent and responsible way.

## Why these principles matter

At CMI, research data management cannot be reduced to one concern alone. It is not only about privacy, not only about security, and not only about open science. Data-related decisions often involve several considerations at once:

- research quality
- participant protection
- legal and ethical obligations
- secure collaboration
- documentation and continuity
- archiving and reuse

A principles-based approach helps researchers and support staff work across these concerns without treating them as separate silos.

## 1. Manage data in ways that support good research

Research data management is part of research quality. Data should be handled in ways that make the research process more understandable, orderly, and traceable.

This means that data should be:

- organised in a clear way
- documented as the project develops
- understandable to others who are authorised to work on the project
- linked to the methods and decisions that shaped it

Poorly managed data creates practical and analytical problems. Files become difficult to interpret, versions are confused, decisions are not traceable, and continuity depends too heavily on individual memory.

## 2. Apply proportionality

Data management measures should be proportionate to the nature of the project and the sensitivity of the material.

Not all projects need the same level of control, documentation, or restriction. At the same time, projects should not rely on minimal routines where the data or context requires more careful handling.

Proportionality means considering questions such as:

- What kinds of data are involved?
- How sensitive is the material?
- How many people need access?
- What are the main risks if data is lost, overshared, misunderstood, or misused?
- What level of documentation is needed for the project to remain understandable and workable?

The aim is neither over-control nor under-management. The aim is a level of care that matches the actual project.

## 3. Protect people, relationships, and context

Research data management should help protect participants, collaborators, and others affected by the research.

At CMI, risk often arises not only from direct identifiers such as names or contact details, but also from context. Political setting, geographic specificity, institutional affiliation, local roles, or combinations of details may make material sensitive even where the file itself appears limited or partially de-identified.

This means that responsible data management should take account of:

- direct and indirect identification
- social, political, and institutional context
- confidentiality commitments
- unequal power relations
- the possible consequences of exposure, even where no formal identifier is present

This principle should shape decisions about collection, access, storage, publication, and sharing.

## 4. Document decisions as you go

Documentation is part of responsible data management, not something added at the end.

Projects should document the decisions that shape the data and make it possible to understand what the material is, how it was created, how it changed, and how it should be interpreted.

This may include:

- descriptions of data types and sources
- collection methods
- file naming and folder structures
- transcription or translation decisions
- processing steps and transformations
- access decisions
- retention, deletion, and sharing decisions

Without documentation, even well-stored files may become difficult to interpret or reuse.

## 5. Match access and security to sensitivity

Security should reflect the data, the project context, and the access needs of the people involved.

This means that projects should think not only about where files are stored, but also about:

- who needs access
- which files they need access to
- whether all team members need the same level of access
- how data is shared internally and externally
- how identifying information is separated where relevant
- how risks linked to travel, fieldwork, devices, or external services are handled

Good security is not only technical. It also depends on clear routines, limited access, and good judgement in everyday work.

## 6. Treat data management as a shared responsibility

Research data management is a shared institutional task.

Researchers are central because they understand the project, the methods, and the material. But they should not be expected to carry all legal, technical, or organisational responsibility alone. Good data management depends on coordination between researchers, project leads, advisers, administration, and technical support.

This principle means that:

- responsibilities should be clarified early
- support functions should be used where needed
- data-related problems should not be handled informally when they require institutional input
- projects should not depend entirely on one individual’s personal routines

Shared responsibility does not remove individual responsibility. It makes it clearer and more workable.

## 7. Be as open as possible, and as closed as necessary

CMI should support openness where this is appropriate, but not treat openness as automatic.

Some projects can share data openly. Others may only be able to share metadata, documentation, code, instruments, or selected outputs. Some data may need restricted access, long embargoes, or no sharing beyond the project team. These differences should be addressed explicitly.

This principle means that projects should avoid both extremes:

- promising openness that the project cannot responsibly deliver
- closing everything by default without considering what could in fact be documented or shared

Responsible openness depends on the actual nature of the material, the conditions under which it was collected, and the risks or obligations attached to it.

## 8. Plan for the full lifecycle

Data-related decisions should not be postponed until the end of the project.

Important choices are made early: what data to collect, how to organise it, how access is structured, how documentation is maintained, and what future sharing or retention may be possible. These decisions affect the whole project lifecycle.

Projects should therefore plan not only for collection and analysis, but also for:

- ongoing documentation
- staff changes and collaboration
- publication and reporting
- retention, archiving, or deletion
- project closure and handover

A lifecycle approach helps avoid last-minute confusion and makes later stages easier to manage.

## What this means in practice

At CMI, these principles should guide practical decisions such as:

- how to set up a project folder
- how to separate identifying information from core research material
- how much documentation a project needs
- whether a dataset can be shared, and in what form
- when to ask for support
- how to prepare material for closure or archiving

The principles do not remove the need for judgement. They provide a common basis for making that judgement more consistent.

## Minimum practice at CMI

At a minimum, projects should aim to ensure that:

- data is organised and understandable
- responsibilities are clear
- access is limited to what is needed
- documentation is maintained during the project
- sensitive material is handled with appropriate care
- future retention, deletion, or sharing is considered early enough to shape practice

## Quick check: applying the principles

Use these questions when making a data-related decision:

1. What is the actual purpose of this data or file?
2. Who really needs access to it?
3. What would be the main risk if it were lost, misunderstood, or shared too widely?
4. Is the level of control proportionate to the sensitivity and context?
5. Has the decision been documented clearly enough for others to understand it later?
6. Could any part of this material be prepared for later sharing, archiving, or preservation?

## Related tools

### Principles checklist

Use this checklist when setting up or reviewing a project:

- Is the level of data management proportionate to the project?
- Are contextual and relational risks considered, not only direct identifiers?
- Is access limited to what is needed?
- Are key decisions being documented as the project develops?
- Are responsibilities clear?
- Has the project considered what may later be shared, archived, retained, or deleted?

### Example prompt for project teams

When discussing data management in a project meeting, ask:

> What are the main kinds of data in this project, what are the main risks around them, and what level of documentation, access control, and future sharing is realistic?
