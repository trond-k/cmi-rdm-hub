# The Research Data Lifecycle
*Trond Kvamme · 4 min read*

## Introduction

The research data lifecycle is a conceptual and operational model that helps researchers, administrators, and support staff understand and manage data across the entire span of a research project — from initial ideas to long-term preservation and beyond. It is not just a technical map but a structured way of thinking about how knowledge is generated, documented, safeguarded, and ultimately shared or retained.

At its core, this lifecycle recognises that data is not a standalone product, but an embedded part of knowledge-making. Each stage of the lifecycle reflects a different set of questions, constraints, responsibilities, and opportunities for reflection or intervention. The model presented here is grounded in the practical workflows and compliance environments researchers operate within — especially in contexts where ethics, privacy, funding, and long-term reuse are central concerns.

---

## Stages and definitions in the research data lifecycle

**1. Frame:** The stage where researchers define a problem space, explore literature, articulate initial questions or hypotheses, and outline ethical or epistemic motivations — before funding is secured. It is the early-stage formulation of research questions, purpose, and ethical scope. This stage may result in preliminary documents such as concept notes, ethical justifications, or stakeholder mappings, and forms the basis for funding applications. The **frame** process is internal and intellectual, as opposed to **fund**, which is external and administrative.

**2. Fund:** The process of aligning the intellectual framing and conceptual groundwork with external funding opportunities and complying with funder requirements, including expectations for openness, ethics, and impact.

**3. Plan:** A procedural and compliance-driven stage where researchers document how data will be managed, shared, and protected. This typically includes Data Management Plans (DMPs) and ethics protocols.

**4. Collect:** The empirical engagement with the field or data sources, involving the active gathering of materials through interviews, surveys, scraping, observation, or other data collection means.

**5. Store:** Focused on where and how data is held during the project; this include short-term, active-use storage that must meet security and accessibility needs.

**6. Process:** The transformation of raw data into analysis-ready formats, often involving cleaning, pseudonymisation, or reformatting. This is a boundary stage between collection and interpretation.

**7. Analyse:** The stage where researchers engage with data to answer questions through modeling, coding, or qualitative interpretation. This is defined by epistemic engagement rather than just data handling.

**8. Publish:** The formal dissemination of outputs - datasets, code, articles - via repositories, journals, or institutional platforms. It emphasizes transparency, reproducibility, and open access when possible.

**9. Preserve:** Concerned with ensuring the long-term safety, integrity, and accessibility of data after the project ends. This includes archiving, metadata curation, and format sustainability.

**10. Discover:** Ensures that outputs can be found by others through persistent identifiers, metadata standards, and indexing strategies. This is the foundation for visibility and reuse.

**11. Access:** The stage where conditions for retrieving and using data are defined and enforced. It involves specifying who can access the data, under what terms, and through which mechanisms — ranging from open availability to tiered or restricted access models.

**12. Share & Reuse:** The stage where data is made available for others to use, adapt, or integrate into new research. It encompasses both the active sharing of data and the design of conditions that support meaningful and responsible reuse, including licensing, documentation, and audience orientation.

---

> **Note:** While these twelve stages are presented as a sequence, research data management is not a strictly linear process. In practice, these stages often overlap, iterate, and inform one another in ways that vary depending on the project context, scope, and data complexity. Data may be collected, processed, and analyzed iteratively. Publishing and preservation strategies may influence planning. Ethical concerns may arise anew at different stages. By treating each phase as a site of both action and reflection, the lifecycle model helps researchers remain aware of their responsibilities and options across the project timeline.

The way these stages unfold depends on the nature of the research, the structure of the project, and external factors such as ethical constraints, collaboration requirements, and funder expectations.

---

## Why a lifecycle approach?

Decisions made early in the project have consequences for later stages, shaping how data can be stored, shared, and ultimately reused. Choices around file formats, documentation practices, metadata standards, and security protocols affect later discoverability, accessibility, and long-term preservation. Anticipating these dependencies - while allowing flexibility to revise and refine earlier decisions as new challenges or insights emerge - is essential for ensuring that research data remains useful, interpretable, and aligned with ethical and legal requirements throughout its lifecycle.

Other advantages of a lifecycle approach:

* **Structured overview:** Dividing research data management into stages may help researchers and research teams to quickly identify **what** needs to be done **when** - ensuring that no essential step is overlooked.

* **Adaptability:** Different disciplines, project sizes, and funding contexts may shift certain details of the cycle or within each stage, but the overarching lifecycle remains applicable and helps maintain consistency.

* **Continuous improvement:** By viewing data management as a cycle, you can incorporate lessons learned from each project phase (or from previous projects), refining your approach and increasing efficiency and compliance over time.
