# Adverse legal environments

*In some countries where you conduct or support research, laws labelled as data protection or cybercrime legislation work, in practice, as instruments of surveillance and control. Where complying with such a law would foreseeably endanger your participants, their safety takes priority over formal compliance with that law. This page helps you recognise these environments and respond to them, and names the people you must involve before acting; you never make this call alone.*

## When protective-sounding laws create risk

A growing number of jurisdictions, including some where CMI works, have enacted data protection, cybercrime, or digital governance legislation that, in design or implementation, serves purposes at odds with protecting the people in your data. The recurring patterns are:

- **Data localisation mandates** that require storing data on domestic servers, placing it within reach of security services rather than shielding it. This is documented across several African and Middle Eastern contexts, where localisation requirements were adopted without safeguards against government intrusion.
- **Broadly defined online offences**, such as 'offensive communication', 'false information', or vaguely worded 'cyberstalking', routinely used against journalists, activists, and researchers.
- **State exemptions**: laws that regulate private actors while granting expansive surveillance powers to state agencies with little meaningful oversight, presenting an outward appearance of rights protection while consolidating control over information flows.
- **Reporting or disclosure obligations** that could expose participants, for example mandatory reporting of 'cybercrimes' defined so broadly that they may cover legitimate research activity or participants' communications.
- **Restrictions on encryption or secure communication** that undermine your ability to protect data in transit or at rest.

## Assess the legal environment early

Build a legal environment assessment into project planning, not just a reading of the statutes. Evaluate how the laws operate in practice: patterns of enforcement, government access to data, and the treatment of researchers, journalists, and civil society actors. Then reassess periodically; a jurisdiction that is stable when your project starts can become more repressive over its course, and data that seemed routine can become dangerous.

## Participant safety comes first

Where compliance with a national law would foreseeably expose participants, communities, or researchers to harm, whether through compelled disclosure, forced localisation, or weakened encryption, prioritise their safety. In practice this can mean declining to store data in-country despite a localisation mandate, using encrypted channels regardless of local restrictions, or limiting the collection of data that could be requisitioned.

Treat formal legal compliance as necessary but not sufficient. A law nominally about 'data protection' or 'cybersecurity' can in practice enable repression, and complying with it does not discharge your ethical responsibilities. Pair legal analysis with a contextual assessment of how the law is actually applied.

!!! danger "Never make this decision alone"
    A choice to depart from formal legal compliance in a given jurisdiction must be principled, documented, and collectively supported. Before acting, raise it with rdm@cmi.no, CMI's DPO, and the research director, and discuss it with your funder, legal counsel, and the relevant ethics bodies. Record the reasoning in your DMP so the decision is defensible later.

## Practical measures in high-risk settings

- Store data in a jurisdiction with stronger protections where a government's track record suggests locally stored data is vulnerable to state access without judicial oversight, and document the decision.
- Use encrypted channels for communication and data transfer, and stay aware of interception risks.
- Manage metadata carefully; who you communicated with, when, and from where can be as revealing as the content itself.
- Collect less. Data that does not exist cannot be requisitioned, so apply data minimisation with extra force in these settings.
- Build in periodic reassessment of the legal and political environment, and be prepared to adapt your practices mid-project.

Classify affected data at the highest sensitivity level and handle it accordingly; see [security and data classification](data-classification.md) and the broader [principles for research data management](rdm-principles.md).

## A note on complexity

None of this implies a blanket disregard for national law, and it does not claim that all data localisation or cybercrime legislation is repressive. Many such laws serve legitimate purposes and reflect genuine efforts to protect citizens' data. The point is that you, with institutional support, must exercise independent judgement informed by context and evidence, rather than treating legal compliance as an automatic proxy for ethical adequacy. In environments where the line between data protection and data extraction is blurred, your primary obligation is to the people whose data you hold.
