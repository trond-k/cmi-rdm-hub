## Ethics and data protection are related but distinct

The [Sikt notification](../guides/sikt-notification.md) is about GDPR compliance — whether your data processing is lawful, proportionate, and transparent. Ethics assessment is about whether your research design is sound — whether the potential for harm has been considered and addressed. A project can be fully GDPR-compliant and still be ethically problematic. Both need attention, but they answer different questions.

This page covers the ethics side: who reviews what at CMI, what you should be thinking through as you design research involving human participants, and how data sensitivity becomes an ethical question rather than just a classification exercise. The Sikt page covers data protection; the [GDPR demystifier](../guides/gdpr-demystifier.md) explains the regulatory framework.

## Ethics review at CMI

### CMI's Research Ethics Committee (REC)

CMI's REC is advisory, not gatekeeping. It does not approve or reject projects — it offers assessments and recommendations. Consultation is voluntary but encouraged for projects involving vulnerable populations, sensitive political contexts, novel data collection methods, or situations where you are uncertain about the ethical implications of your design.

The REC was established in 2019 and consists of at least three CMI researchers. Its advisory model reflects CMI's trust in researcher judgement, but it also means that the ethical responsibility sits squarely with you as the researcher. The REC can help you think through difficult questions — it cannot make those questions go away by issuing an approval stamp.

??? tip "Consult the REC early for complex projects"
    For projects involving high-risk participants, ethically novel methods, or contexts where the standard approaches feel inadequate, consulting the REC during the planning stage — not after the design is locked — gives you the most useful input. Their perspective can strengthen both the ethics design and the Sikt notification.

### REK (Regional Committees for Medical and Health Research Ethics)

REK approval is required for medical and health research under the Health Research Act. Most CMI research is social science *about* health, poverty, or governance — not health research in the regulatory sense. The test is specific: does the project aim to generate new knowledge about health and disease using clinical interventions, biological material, or health registries? A study of how health policy affects marginalised communities is social science research that involves health topics. A clinical trial of a health intervention is health research.

If you are uncertain, submit a *framleggingsvurdering* — a request for REK to assess whether the project falls under their mandate. This is a low-cost way to resolve the question.

### External requirements

Horizon Europe requires an ethics self-assessment in all applications. Partner institutions — universities especially — may require IRB or ethics committee approval on their side, and their timelines can be long. For multi-partner projects, clarify early what approvals each partner needs, who is responsible for obtaining them, and how long the process takes. A partner's ethics committee requiring revisions to the information letter six weeks before fieldwork is a planning failure, not a surprise.

## Designing research that protects participants

Ethics review processes are procedural. The substantive question is whether your research design has genuinely reckoned with the risks it creates for participants. For CMI, this question is sharper than for most institutions — the research profile involves refugees, conflict-affected communities, political dissidents, economically marginalised populations, and people living under authoritarian governance. The questions below are not a checklist. They are the thinking that should underpin every design decision involving human participants.

### Who are the participants, and what are the risks?

The foreseeable risks in CMI research contexts include psychological distress (from revisiting traumatic experiences), social stigma (from association with the research topic), legal exposure (from disclosing information that could be used against them), and physical danger (from being identified as a source in sensitive political research). The ethical question is whether the research benefits are proportionate to these risks — and whether the design minimises harm without compromising the research.

This is not abstract. A project studying political opposition in an authoritarian state exposes participants to risks that a project studying municipal budgeting in Norway does not. The research methods might be identical — semi-structured interviews — but the ethical obligations are fundamentally different. Design should reflect this.

### How will participants be informed?

Ethical consent is always required at CMI, regardless of the GDPR lawful basis. The information letter is the primary instrument, but "informing" is not the same as "handing over a document." In many CMI fieldwork contexts — low literacy settings, contexts where documents carry distrust, situations where the research topic itself is sensitive — oral information delivery is more appropriate, more respectful, and more effective than a written form.

The [informing participants](../guides/informing-participants.md) page has CMI's template and detailed guidance on adapting it to different contexts, literacy levels, and languages. The key planning-stage question is: how will you ensure that participants genuinely understand what they are consenting to, given the specific conditions of your fieldwork?

### Power and positionality

Researcher-participant power imbalances are a structural feature of CMI's research contexts, not an exception. A Norwegian researcher interviewing a refugee about their asylum experience, a senior academic interviewing a village leader through a local gatekeeper, an enumerator employed by a partner organisation surveying households that depend on that organisation's services — these are not equal encounters, and the consent process needs to acknowledge that.

Gatekeeper-facilitated access — a village leader, a community organisation, or a government office introducing the researcher — does not replace individual informed participation. Research assistants need explicit briefing on ethics, not just methods. These are design questions, not afterthoughts, and they belong in the planning stage.

### When documentation itself creates risk

In some CMI fieldwork contexts, the standard documentation practices create their own dangers. A signed consent form linking a participant's name to research on political opposition in an authoritarian state can become evidence. A list of interviewees stored on a device that could be confiscated at a border crossing is a liability, not a safeguard.

Oral consent with no written record of participant identity may be the more ethical choice. Recording decisions, storage choices, and even the decision to collect certain data at all should be weighed against the realistic risk to participants. The institutional guidance addresses this specifically: [fieldwork data security](../../institutional/fieldwork.md) on high-risk contexts, and [information letters and consent](../../institutional/consent-and-information.md) on oral information delivery.

## Data sensitivity as an ethical question

Data classification — Green, Yellow, Red, Black — is partly a technical question about storage infrastructure and access controls. But it is also an ethical question. The technical question is "can we store this securely?" The ethical question is "should we collect this at all?"

Even data that can be stored and protected creates obligations — to participants who entrusted you with their stories, to communities whose experiences are encoded in your transcripts, and to future researchers who may access the data in a context you cannot predict. A dataset of interviews with political dissidents, deposited in a repository and accessed ten years later by a researcher with different values or in a changed political landscape, carries risks that the original researcher may not have foreseen.

This is where CMI's principle that "data is voice" becomes operational. Whose voice are you preserving, and on what terms? Did participants understand that their words might be archived and accessed by others? Is the benefit of preservation — for scholarship, for institutional memory, for the communities represented — proportionate to the risks?

These questions do not have formulaic answers. They require judgement, and the planning stage is when that judgement should be exercised — not during fieldwork when the recording is already running, and not at the end of the project when the archiving decision feels like an administrative task.

## When to do the assessment

During planning, before the Sikt notification. The ethics thinking informs several Sikt form fields — the description of participants and risks, the justification for special category data, the information delivery method, the assessment of whether a DPIA is needed. A researcher who has thought carefully about participant protection before opening the Sikt form will find the form straightforward. One who has not will find it confusing.

For projects with complex ethical dimensions, consult the REC early. Their input strengthens both the research design and the regulatory documentation. The [ethics guidelines catalogue](../guides/ethics-guidelines.md) provides an overview of the national and international frameworks that may be relevant to your project.
