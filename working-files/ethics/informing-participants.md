# Informing Research Participants
*Trond Kvamme · 6 min read*

## Purpose of this guide

This segment provides background and information on how to inform research participants in ways that are clear, ethical, and legally sound. Informing participants is not just a matter of handing over a letter; it is about making sure people understand what they are being asked to take part in, how their data will be used, and what rights they have. This can happen through written materials, oral explanations, or both, depending on the situation and the needs of the participants.

## Transparency is a legal and ethical obligation

Both the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) and standard research ethics require that participants are given clear, accessible information about:

- What the research is about
- What participation involves
- How personal data will be used and protected
- What rights participants have, including the right to withdraw

This obligation to inform participants applies regardless of whether the information is delivered in writing, verbally, or through other formats (e.g. audio, visual aids, interpreter-assisted communication). The goal is not simply to distribute information - it is to ensure that participants have understood it.

### CMI Information Letter Template

CMI's **Information Letter Template** is designed to support transparency. It can be used:

- As a written document (in full or adapted) provided before or during participation
- As a script or reference for oral explanations, particularly in interviews or informal conversations
- As a modular tool to help structure how you explain key issues to different groups

You are encouraged to adapt the format and language of the template to fit the context of your project. This includes adjusting for:

- Low literacy levels or illiteracy (e.g., using oral consent and verbal explanation instead of written documents)
- Children or young people (e.g., using simplified language or engaging guardians)
- Cultural and linguistic differences (e.g., working with translators or using culturally relevant examples)
- Sensitive or high-risk contexts (e.g., giving more time for discussion, avoiding written materials that could compromise anonymity)

### Legal basis: From consent to public interest

Researchers often assume that they must obtain formal consent to comply with the GDPR. In fact, public interest is the recommended legal basis for most research conducted at CMI and similar institutions. Here's how it works:

- **Legal basis for data processing:** CMI uses GDPR Article 6(1)(e) -- processing necessary for a task carried out in the public interest -- as the default basis for processing personal data. This reflects CMI's status as an independent, non-profit research institute whose work serves societal goals.

- **Sensitive data:** If your project involves sensitive categories of data (e.g. political opinions, ethnicity, health), Article 9(2)(j) provides a lawful basis for processing such data for scientific research, subject to appropriate safeguards.

- **Ethical participation:** Even when consent is not the legal basis under the GDPR, participants must still voluntarily agree to take part. This is an ethical requirement, not a legal one -- but it must still be respected and clearly documented.

## What should participants be informed about?

What participants need to know depends on the context. The GDPR lists several elements that must be communicated to participants, but how this is done -- and which details are included -- should be adapted to the setting. What matters most is that participants understand what they are being asked to do, how their data will be used, and that they are free to say yes or no.

### What must always be included (in some form)

Regardless of setting, participants must understand enough to make a voluntary and informed decision. This usually includes:

- What the project is about
- Why they are being asked
- What participation involves (e.g. what they'll do, how long it will take)
- That participation is voluntary, and they can withdraw

In almost all cases, this can be explained briefly and orally, with an offer to answer follow-up questions.

### What should be included when feasible or appropriate

These depend on the participant's situation and level of risk:

- **How their data will be protected:** A general reassurance like "your data will be kept confidential and handled securely" is often sufficient orally.

- **Legal basis for data processing:** This is legally required, but how explicitly it's stated can vary. In most interviews, it's fine to say, "This research is carried out in the public interest". You don't need to say "Article 6(1)(e)" unless the participant wants more detail.

- **Data subject rights:** A short statement like, "You can contact us if you have questions or want to access your data" is often enough. A full list can be included in written letters or follow-up materials.

- **Contact info:** This must be provided, but again, it doesn't have to be printed unless needed. You can say, "You can reach me at this email if you want to follow up".

### When it might be inappropriate or even risky to include everything

In some settings, giving too much detail can cause confusion, mistrust, or even risk to the participant, for example when working with non-literate or low-literacy groups, participants in authoritarian or repressive settings, children or youth participants, or highly informal or time-limited conversations (e.g. street interviews).

In these cases it is recommended to use simple, plain language, focus on core messages about what's happening and their rights, avoid overloading with technical legal terms (e.g. "data controller," "legal basis," "Article 9(2)(j)"), and instead offer to follow up later or provide information in another format if needed.

The layered structure of the template can help -- starting with a short summary and offering more detailed information as needed.

## Why legal consent is problematic in research

Under the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR), consent is one of several legal bases for processing personal data. But it comes with strict legal requirements. It should be:

- **Freely given** -- The person must not feel pressured or coerced.
- **Specific** -- Consent must relate to a clearly defined and limited purpose.
- **Informed** -- The person must fully understand what they're agreeing to.
- **Unambiguous** -- Consent must be given through a clear, affirmative action.
- **Withdrawable** -- The person must be able to withdraw consent at any time, and doing so must be as easy as giving it.

These requirements are appropriate in many everyday settings -- for example, when signing up for a newsletter, downloading an app, or participating in a medical trial. But in social science research, and especially qualitative or exploratory projects, applying GDPR consent as the legal basis can be legally fragile and practically misleading. Here are a few reasons:

- **Long-term or evolving research questions:** Qualitative research often evolves as the project develops. New themes may emerge during interviews or fieldwork, and the final use of the data (e.g., in publications or secondary analysis) might not be known in advance. This makes it difficult to meet the GDPR requirement for consent to be "specific and informed."

- **Participants may misunderstand the role of consent:** If researchers use consent as the legal basis, participants may assume that they have the right to withdraw their data at any time -- including after analysis or publication. However, GDPR only grants that right while the person is still identifiable and while consent remains the legal basis. Once data has been anonymised or the legal basis is public interest, this right does not apply in the same way. This can create false expectations and potentially erode trust.

- **Power imbalances or unclear voluntariness:** In some research contexts -- e.g., involving marginalised groups, or in certain political settings -- participants may feel they ought to agree. This compromises the "freely given" aspect of consent. Using "public interest" as the legal basis allows researchers to focus instead on ethical voluntariness -- meaning the person freely chooses whether to participate, without over-legalising the situation.

- **Withdrawal and record-keeping problems:** Under GDPR consent, if someone withdraws, researchers are generally obliged to delete all personal data collected about them. This is often not feasible in qualitative research, where transcripts are already coded, interpreted, or published -- especially if the data cannot be fully separated. Using "public interest" avoids this problem, while still allowing participants to opt out in ways that are appropriate and explained from the outset.

### In summary

Using "public interest" as your legal basis allows you to:

- Be transparent with participants about how their data will be used
- Design flexible, evolving research projects without risking non-compliance
- Uphold ethical participation without overstating legal rights
- Reduce confusion and ensure that data protection responsibilities are realistic

Participants still have rights (access, correction, objection), and ethical consent -- the right to freely say yes or no to participation -- remains just as important. But legal consent under GDPR is not the right tool for most of the research conducted at CMI.

### Glossary: What do some of these terms mean?

*An overview of terms written in clear, neutral language, without legal jargon. The glossary can be included at the end of the information letter or provided orally or in writing if participants ask questions about specific terms during interviews, meetings, or consent discussions.*

**Personal data:** Any information that can be linked to you personally -- for example, your name, voice, email address, job title, or anything else that could identify you.

**Sensitive personal data:** A special category of personal data that includes things like political opinions, religious beliefs, ethnic background, health information, or trade union membership. These types of data are protected more strictly by law.

**Data processing:** Any action involving personal data -- such as collecting, recording, storing, analysing, sharing, or deleting it.

**Voluntary participation:** You choose freely whether to join the research. You can say no or withdraw later, without giving a reason and without any negative consequences.

**Anonymisation:** Removing all identifying details from data so that no one -- not even the researchers -- can tell who the information came from.

**Pseudonymisation:** Replacing your name or identifying details with a code. A separate list links the code to your identity, but only authorised people can access it.

**Legal basis:** The rule in the law that gives the research team permission to use your personal data. At CMI, this is usually "public interest" -- which means the research is done for the benefit of society, not for profit.

**Public interest:** A legal term used when research is done to improve knowledge or inform policy in a way that benefits the public. It allows researchers to collect and use data without needing GDPR consent, as long as safeguards are in place.

**Consent (ethical):** Your agreement to take part in the project. This is voluntary and based on clear information. Even when GDPR consent is not required, your participation must always be voluntary.

**Data controller:** The organisation responsible for deciding how and why your personal data is used. For this project, that is Chr. Michelsen Institute (CMI).

**Data processor:** A company or person who handles data on behalf of the main research organisation (e.g., a transcription service that types up interview recordings).

**Withdrawal:** The right to stop participating in the project. You can usually also ask to have your data deleted -- unless it has already been anonymised or archived.

**Archiving:** Storing research data for long-term use, so that others can check the findings or build on them in future studies. Only de-identified data is archived.

**GDPR:** Short for the General Data Protection Regulation -- a European law that protects your personal data and gives you certain rights over how it is used.
