# GDPR De-mystifier

A plain-language reference to GDPR concepts as they apply to CMI research. Each entry explains what the concept means, why it matters, and — where relevant — what CMI's interpretive position is.

This is not a legal textbook. It is a working reference for researchers filling in Sikt notifications, writing information letters, or trying to understand what a DPO is asking them.

Entries are grouped thematically, not alphabetically, because the concepts make more sense in relation to each other.


## Where the GDPR comes from — and why it matters for research

### A human rights instrument, not a bureaucratic one

The GDPR did not appear from nowhere. It is the latest expression of
a European tradition that treats personal data protection as a
fundamental right — not a consumer preference, not a compliance
checkbox, but a right on the same footing as freedom of expression
and freedom of assembly.

The lineage runs through the Council of Europe's Convention 108 (1981),
the EU Charter of Fundamental Rights (Article 8: "Everyone has the
right to the protection of personal data concerning him or her"), and
the earlier Data Protection Directive (1995). The GDPR, which took
effect in 2018, replaced the Directive with a regulation that applies
directly and uniformly across the EU and EEA.

The driving context was the explosion of large-scale, commercial data
processing. By the 2010s, technology companies, data brokers, social
media platforms, and advertising networks were collecting, combining,
profiling, and trading personal data on an industrial scale — often
with minimal transparency and no meaningful consent. The GDPR was
designed to reassert individual control over personal data in this
landscape: to force organisations that process data about people to
justify why, explain how, limit what they collect, secure what they
hold, and delete what they no longer need.

This origin matters for researchers, because it explains both why the
regulation exists and why much of its language and machinery can feel
ill-fitted to research. The GDPR's default mental model is a
technology company processing millions of customer records for
commercial purposes. A researcher conducting 25 interviews in rural
Zambia is operating in the same legal framework, but at a completely
different scale, with completely different risks, and for a completely
different purpose. The regulation recognises this. The question is how.

### Proportionality: the principle that makes the GDPR workable for research

The GDPR is built on the principle of proportionality — and it says
so explicitly. [Recital 4](https://gdpr-info.eu/recitals/no-4/)
states that the right to personal data protection "is not an absolute
right; it must be considered in relation to its function in society
and be balanced against other fundamental rights, in accordance with
the principle of proportionality." This is not a loophole or an
exception. It is a structural feature of the regulation. Every
obligation in the GDPR is meant to be read through it.

Proportionality means that the safeguards you apply should match the
actual risk of the processing, not the theoretical maximum. A
national health registry containing millions of medical records
requires industrial-grade security, strict access controls, and
formal impact assessments. A qualitative study with 15 interview
participants requires care, good practice, and sensible precautions
— but not the same machinery. Both are covered by the GDPR. Both
must comply. But what compliance looks like is proportional to the
risk.

For research specifically, the GDPR goes further. Scientific research
is recognised throughout the regulation as a legitimate and valued
societal purpose. [Recitals 156](https://gdpr-info.eu/recitals/no-156/)–[159](https://gdpr-info.eu/recitals/no-159/)
set out a framework of research-specific accommodations.
[Article 89](https://gdpr-info.eu/art-89-gdpr/) establishes that
personal data processing for research purposes is subject to
"appropriate safeguards" — not maximum safeguards, but appropriate
ones — and allows member states to provide derogations from certain
data subject rights when processing is for research. The regulation
explicitly acknowledges that research is exploratory, that data needs
may not be fully specifiable in advance, and that retention for
verification and reuse serves the public interest.

This is not a grudging concession. The GDPR actively supports
research. It simply asks that research, like any other processing,
be conducted with respect for the people whose data is involved.

### The Norwegian implementation

Norway is not an EU member state, but the GDPR applies in Norway
through the EEA Agreement. The Norwegian implementation is the
**Personal Data Act** (*Personopplysningsloven*, 2018), which
incorporates the GDPR into Norwegian law and adds national provisions
where the regulation permits member-state discretion.

The most important national provisions for CMI researchers are:

**Section 8** provides the legal basis for processing personal data
in the public interest, including scientific research. This is the
national-law anchor for using public interest
([Article 6(1)(e)](https://gdpr-info.eu/art-6-gdpr/)) as the lawful
basis for research — and it is why public interest is the standard
basis for research projects in Norway, not a CMI-specific choice.

**Section 9** implements the derogation for special category data
processing for research purposes
([Article 9(2)(j)](https://gdpr-info.eu/art-9-gdpr/)), specifying
the safeguards required under Norwegian law.

**Section 17** implements the research-specific modifications to
data subject rights permitted by
[Article 89(2)](https://gdpr-info.eu/art-89-gdpr/). Under §17, the
rights to access, rectification, restriction, and objection may be
limited when processing is for research purposes, provided that the
research serves the public interest and appropriate safeguards are
in place. This is the provision that allows researchers to retain
data when a participant withdraws, where deletion would seriously
impair the research — though CMI's default practice is to
accommodate deletion requests unless there is a documented,
compelling reason not to.

**Section 26** provides for processing of personal data for archiving
purposes in the public interest, which supports the long-term
retention and sharing of research data.

The **Norwegian Data Protection Authority** (*Datatilsynet*) is the
supervisory authority responsible for enforcing the GDPR in Norway.
**Sikt** — the Norwegian Agency for Shared Services in Education
and Research — provides data protection advisory services
specifically for the research sector, including the notification
form that CMI researchers use.

### What this means in practice

When you sit down with the Sikt notification form, or draft an
information letter, or decide how to handle a participant's request
to withdraw — you are working within a framework that was built to
regulate Facebook and Google, but that explicitly and deliberately
accommodates scientific research. The regulation asks you to be
thoughtful, transparent, and careful with people's data. It does not
ask you to treat a 20-person interview study as if it were a
surveillance operation.

The concepts explained below are the building blocks of this
framework. Where the GDPR's language is technical or its implications
for research are non-obvious, this guide translates. Where CMI has
taken a specific interpretive position — always within the space the
regulation provides — this guide says so and explains why.


## Roles: who is responsible for what

### Data controller

The institution that decides *why* and *how* personal data is processed.
For CMI projects, CMI is the data controller. This means CMI — not the
individual researcher — carries legal responsibility for GDPR compliance.
The PI acts on behalf of CMI.

Why it matters: the Sikt notification asks who the controller is. For
single-institution CMI projects, the answer is always CMI. It gets more
interesting when partners are involved (see *joint controllers* and
*data processor* below).


### Joint controllers

Two or more institutions that *together* decide why and how personal
data is processed. This is the GDPR term
([Article 26](https://gdpr-info.eu/art-26-gdpr/)) for a genuine
research partnership where both institutions shape the research
design, collect data, and conduct analysis.

CMI's position: joint controllership is the natural framing for CMI's
international research collaborations. It reflects the reality of equal
partnership better than the alternative (one controller, one processor),
and it requires a joint controllership agreement — a lighter and more
appropriate instrument than Standard Contractual Clauses. See
*joint controllership agreement* below.

Not every collaboration is joint controllership. If you hire a local
firm to run a survey to your specifications, that firm is a *data
processor*, not a joint controller.


### Data processor

An organisation that processes personal data *on your instructions*,
without deciding the purpose or design of the research. Typical
examples for CMI: a contracted survey firm, a transcription service,
a cloud platform that stores your data.

Why it matters: if you use a data processor, you need a *data
processing agreement* (DPA) under
[Article 28](https://gdpr-info.eu/art-28-gdpr/). The processor has
no independent right to use the data — they do what you tell them
and nothing more.

The test: does the other party decide what to do with the data, or
do they follow your instructions? If the former, they are a controller
(or joint controller). If the latter, they are a processor.


### Data subject

The person whose personal data you are processing. In CMI research,
this is usually a research participant — an interviewee, a survey
respondent, someone observed during fieldwork. It can also be a
*third person*: someone your participant talks about who becomes
identifiable in the data.

Data subjects have rights under GDPR (see *data subject rights*
below), regardless of whether they are in the EU.


### Data Protection Officer (DPO)

A person designated to advise an institution on data protection
compliance ([Article 37](https://gdpr-info.eu/art-37-gdpr/)). Not
all institutions are required to have one. CMI's data protection
contact serves a similar advisory function for researchers. In Sikt
correspondence, the DPO or data protection contact is listed as the
institutional point of contact.


## Legal basis: why you are allowed to process the data

### Lawful basis (Article 6)

GDPR requires a legal justification — a "lawful basis" — for every
instance of personal data processing
([Article 6](https://gdpr-info.eu/art-6-gdpr/)). There are six
possible bases. For research, only two are commonly relevant:
*public interest* and *consent*.

You must choose one before you start collecting data. The choice
has consequences for the rest of the project: what rights participants
have, what your information letter says, what happens if someone
withdraws.


### Public interest (Article 6(1)(e))

The lawful basis that says: processing is necessary to perform a task
carried out in the public interest. Scientific research is recognised
as a public interest task under Norwegian law (Personal Data Act §8)
and GDPR ([Article 89](https://gdpr-info.eu/art-89-gdpr/)).

This is the standard lawful basis for research projects in Norway.
Sikt's own guidance states that public interest is often more
appropriate than consent for research. It is the default for CMI
projects — qualitative, quantitative, interviews, surveys, fieldwork,
evaluations.

What it does *not* mean: it does not mean participants have no say.
They still give *ethical consent* to participate (see below). It
means the *legal* justification for processing their data is the
public interest in research, not their individual permission.


### GDPR consent (Article 6(1)(a))

A lawful basis where the data subject's explicit agreement is the
legal justification for processing
([Article 7](https://gdpr-info.eu/art-7-gdpr/) sets out the
conditions). GDPR consent has strict requirements: it must be freely
given, specific, informed, unambiguous, documented, and as easy to
withdraw as to give. Withdrawal triggers a legal obligation to delete
the data.

CMI's position: GDPR consent is *not* the default basis for CMI
research. It is reserved for specific, bounded processing activities
where it is the most natural fit — such as consent to be recorded,
consent to be named in publications, or consent to have data archived
for future reuse. These are layered on top of the public interest
basis, not used instead of it.

Why not consent as the default? Withdrawal instability (withdrawal
means deletion, which can impair ongoing research); specificity
problems (qualitative research is exploratory); and the risk of
conflating legal consent with ethical consent, which confuses
participants and researchers alike.


### Ethical consent

Not a GDPR term. This is the research ethics obligation — grounded in
the NESH guidelines and international research ethics norms — to ensure
that participation is voluntary, informed, and revocable.

Ethical consent is *always* required at CMI, regardless of which GDPR
lawful basis you use. It is what your information letter facilitates
and what your participant agrees to when they decide to take part.

The crucial distinction: a participant who withdraws their *ethical*
consent is withdrawing from the research. A participant who withdraws
*GDPR consent* is triggering a legal obligation to delete their data.
Under the public interest basis, only ethical consent is in play —
so withdrawal means the person stops participating, but their existing
data can be retained if deletion would seriously impair the research
(though CMI's default is to accommodate deletion requests).


## Personal data: what counts

### Personal data

Any information relating to an identified or identifiable natural
person ([Article 4(1)](https://gdpr-info.eu/art-4-gdpr/)).
"Identifiable" is broad: a name is personal data, but so is a
combination of age, occupation, village, and family size if that
combination could single someone out.

For CMI researchers: if you could, in principle, work out who someone
is from the data you hold — even by combining it with other information
you have — the data is personal data and GDPR applies.


### Special category data (Article 9)

A subset of personal data that GDPR considers particularly sensitive
([Article 9](https://gdpr-info.eu/art-9-gdpr/)): racial or ethnic
origin, political opinions, religious or philosophical beliefs, trade
union membership, genetic data, biometric data, health data, data
about sex life or sexual orientation.

Processing special category data requires an *additional* legal basis
on top of your [Article 6](https://gdpr-info.eu/art-6-gdpr/) basis.
For CMI research, this is
[Article 9(2)(j)](https://gdpr-info.eu/art-9-gdpr/): processing
necessary for scientific research purposes with appropriate safeguards.

CMI's position: special category data is common in CMI research and
often arises incidentally — a participant mentions a health condition,
or the research context inherently involves ethnicity. The threshold
is *processing*, not *intent*: if the data is captured, Article 9
applies. But context matters. A passing mention of attending a mosque
in an interview about municipal budgets is not necessarily "religious
beliefs" in a GDPR-relevant sense. The question is whether the data
is being processed *in relation to* the special category.

Practical rule: if your research topic involves governance, conflict,
migration, health, ethnicity, or political participation, assume you
will process at least one special category. Flag it early in the Sikt
notification and the data inventory.


### Biometric data

Data resulting from specific technical processing of physical,
physiological, or behavioural characteristics that allows unique
identification ([Article 4(14)](https://gdpr-info.eu/art-4-gdpr/)).
The most relevant case for CMI: **voice recordings**. A voice on an
audio recording is arguably biometric data because it can identify a
person. This is why CMI treats recording consent as a separate,
specific consent element.

Photographs can also constitute biometric data if processed through
facial recognition, but a photograph in a research context is not
automatically biometric data.


### Pseudonymisation

Replacing direct identifiers (names, ID numbers) with a code, while
keeping a separate key that links the code back to the person
([Article 4(5)](https://gdpr-info.eu/art-4-gdpr/)). The data is
still personal data — it can be re-identified using the key. But
pseudonymisation reduces risk: if the research dataset is exposed,
the identities are not immediately revealed.

The Sikt form asks whether you store identifiable data separately.
If you use pseudonyms and keep the key in a separate, restricted
location, the answer is yes.

Not to be confused with *anonymisation* (see below).


### Anonymisation

Removing or transforming personal data so that the individual can
no longer be identified, even by combining the remaining data with
other available information. Truly anonymised data is *no longer
personal data* and falls outside GDPR entirely
([Recital 26](https://gdpr-info.eu/recitals/no-26/)).

Anonymisation is harder than it sounds, especially for qualitative
data. A transcript with the name removed but detailed descriptions
of a person's role, location, family, and experiences may still be
identifiable — particularly in small communities or specialised
professional networks. Anonymisation requires thinking about
*combinations* of information, not just individual identifiers.

The Sikt form asks what happens to data at project end.
"Anonymisation" means you delete the linkage key and strip or
generalise any remaining identifiers so the data cannot be
re-identified. This is the standard endpoint for CMI projects.


### De-identification

Not a GDPR term, but widely used (especially in US and health research
contexts) to mean removing obvious identifiers from data. In practice,
de-identification sits somewhere between pseudonymisation and full
anonymisation. It is less rigorous than GDPR anonymisation — data
that has been "de-identified" in a loose sense may still be personal
data under GDPR if re-identification is reasonably possible.

If you encounter this term in funder requirements or partner
agreements, clarify what is actually meant. For GDPR purposes, the
relevant distinction is between pseudonymised data (still personal
data, still under GDPR) and anonymised data (no longer personal data,
outside GDPR).


## Principles: the rules of the road

### Data minimisation (Article 5(1)(c))

The principle that you should collect only the personal data that is
adequate, relevant, and necessary for your research purpose
([Article 5(1)(c)](https://gdpr-info.eu/art-5-gdpr/)).

CMI's position: data minimisation does not mean restricting interviews
to a pre-approved script or refusing to listen when a participant
shares information beyond the interview guide. Qualitative research
is inherently open-ended. What minimisation *does* require is
intentionality (collect data related to your research purpose),
no gratuitous collection (don't gather data you clearly don't need),
and proportional retention (don't keep identifiable data longer than
necessary).

A well-designed project that collects rich data from a clearly defined
group for a clearly articulated purpose is consistent with data
minimisation, even if the dataset is extensive.


### Purpose limitation (Article 5(1)(b))

Personal data must be collected for specified, explicit, and legitimate
purposes and not processed in a way incompatible with those purposes
([Article 5(1)(b)](https://gdpr-info.eu/art-5-gdpr/)). However, GDPR
explicitly provides that further processing for scientific research
purposes is *not* considered incompatible with the original purpose
(Article 5(1)(b), second sentence).

What this means for CMI: you should define your research purpose
clearly, but you are not locked into a narrow reading. If your project
evolves — new research questions emerge from the data, a follow-up
study builds on the original — further processing for research is
permitted, provided safeguards are in place. If data will be archived
for genuinely open-ended future reuse, specific consent for archiving
is the cleaner approach.


### Storage limitation (Article 5(1)(e))

Personal data should be kept in identifiable form no longer than
necessary for the research purpose
([Article 5(1)(e)](https://gdpr-info.eu/art-5-gdpr/)). After that,
it should be anonymised or deleted.

CMI's default: personal data is retained for the project duration
plus a verification period (10 years for primary research data,
5 years for administrative records), after which it is anonymised
or deleted. Data archived in a repository for reuse is governed by
the repository's policies.

The Sikt form asks for a project end date, which is the date by
which data will be anonymised or deleted — not the date the last
paper is published.


### Accountability (Article 5(2))

The controller must be able to demonstrate compliance with GDPR
principles ([Article 5(2)](https://gdpr-info.eu/art-5-gdpr/)). This
is why documentation matters: the Sikt notification, the data
management plan, the information letter, agreements with partners
and processors, and records of data handling decisions.

You do not need a perfect paper trail. You need enough documentation
that, if asked, you can show what you did with personal data and why.


## Safeguards and security

### Appropriate safeguards (Article 89(1))

GDPR requires "appropriate technical and organisational measures" when
processing personal data for research
([Article 89(1)](https://gdpr-info.eu/art-89-gdpr/)). What counts as
"appropriate" is — deliberately — not specified in detail. It depends
on the risk.

For most CMI projects, appropriate safeguards include: encrypted
storage (provided by M365 and TSD), access restricted to the project
team, pseudonymisation where practicable, secure data transmission,
and anonymisation or deletion when the research purpose is fulfilled.

The key word is *appropriate*, not *maximum*. A qualitative project
with 20 interview participants does not need the same security
architecture as a national health registry.


### Encryption

Transforming data so it cannot be read without a key. Two forms
matter for CMI researchers:

**Encryption at rest**: data is encrypted when stored on a disk or
server. CMI's M365 environment and TSD both provide this
automatically. Your files are encrypted even if you do nothing —
but only on institutional infrastructure, not on an unencrypted USB
stick.

**Encryption in transit**: data is encrypted while being transmitted
over a network. HTTPS, the standard for web traffic, provides this.
Uploading files to M365 or TSD uses encrypted transit. Sending an
unencrypted email attachment does not.

For fieldwork: ensure devices used for data collection (laptops,
tablets, recorders) have device-level encryption enabled. This is
the single most important fieldwork security measure.


### Data Processing Agreement (DPA)

A contract between a data controller and a data processor that
specifies what the processor may do with the data, what security
measures they must apply, and what happens when the processing ends.
Required by [Article 28](https://gdpr-info.eu/art-28-gdpr/) whenever
you use an external service that handles personal data on your behalf.

Common CMI triggers: hiring a transcription service, using a
contracted survey firm, or using a cloud tool that is not part of
CMI's institutional infrastructure. If the service touches personal
data, you need a DPA. Many commercial services (e.g., major survey
platforms) offer standard DPAs — check before signing up.


### Joint controllership agreement (Article 26)

An agreement between joint controllers that defines each party's
responsibilities: who handles data subject requests, who maintains
the information letter, who is responsible for security, who deletes
data at the end. Required by
[Article 26](https://gdpr-info.eu/art-26-gdpr/) whenever two
institutions share decision-making over personal data processing.

CMI's position: this is the preferred instrument for international
research collaborations where both CMI and the partner institution
shape the research. It is lighter and more appropriate than Standard
Contractual Clauses for genuinely collaborative relationships.


### Standard Contractual Clauses (SCCs)

Pre-approved contract templates issued by the European Commission for
transferring personal data to countries outside the EEA that lack an
adequacy decision ([Article 46(2)(c)](https://gdpr-info.eu/art-46-gdpr/)).
They impose detailed obligations on both parties regarding data
protection.

CMI's position: SCCs are designed for commercial data processing
relationships and are often disproportionate for academic research
collaborations. CMI prefers joint controllership agreements or the
research derogation for international research partnerships, reserving
SCCs for sustained data-sharing arrangements where other mechanisms
are not suitable.


### Data Protection Impact Assessment (DPIA)

A structured assessment of the risks that data processing poses to
data subjects, and the measures taken to mitigate those risks
([Article 35](https://gdpr-info.eu/art-35-gdpr/)). Required when
processing is "likely to result in a high risk" to individuals —
typically triggered when two or more risk factors are present
(special category data, vulnerable groups, large scale, etc.).

The Sikt notification form includes a DPIA section. Many CMI projects
will trigger it, because CMI research commonly involves special
category data and vulnerable groups. A DPIA is not a barrier — it
is a structured way of showing that you have thought about the risks
and taken reasonable steps. The principle is proportionality: describe
the risks honestly and explain the safeguards. You do not need to
eliminate all risk.


## Data transfers: moving data across borders

### Third-country transfer

Any movement of personal data to a country outside the EU/EEA. This
includes sending data to a partner, storing data on a server in a
non-EEA country, and — technically — collecting data on a device
during fieldwork abroad.

Most CMI projects involve third-country transfers. The GDPR requires
a legal mechanism for each transfer (Chapter V,
[Articles 44](https://gdpr-info.eu/art-44-gdpr/)–[49](https://gdpr-info.eu/art-49-gdpr/)).
The three main options are described below.


### Adequacy decision (Article 45)

A finding by the European Commission that a country provides an
adequate level of data protection
([Article 45](https://gdpr-info.eu/art-45-gdpr/)), so data can flow
there without additional safeguards. Countries with adequacy decisions
include the UK, Japan, South Korea, Canada (commercial organisations),
and a few others.

Most CMI fieldwork countries do not have adequacy decisions. If your
partner is in a country that does, this is the simplest transfer
mechanism.


### Appropriate safeguards (Article 46)

Transfer mechanisms that provide legal safeguards in the absence of
an adequacy decision ([Article 46](https://gdpr-info.eu/art-46-gdpr/)).
The most common are Standard Contractual Clauses and Binding Corporate
Rules. For CMI, a joint controllership agreement with contractual data
protection provisions also falls here.


### Research derogation (Article 49(1)(d))

A provision allowing transfers that are "necessary for important
reasons of public interest," which includes scientific research
([Article 49(1)(d)](https://gdpr-info.eu/art-49-gdpr/)). This is a
legitimate basis for transferring research data to non-EEA countries,
though it should not be used as a blanket exemption — document why
the specific transfer is necessary for your research.

CMI's position: the research derogation is a practical and
proportionate mechanism for many CMI fieldwork and collaboration
transfers, particularly where the alternative would be SCCs for
every partner in every country.


## Rights: what participants can ask for

### Data subject rights

GDPR gives individuals a set of rights over their personal data.
Under the public interest basis, some of these rights are modified
for research ([Article 89(2)](https://gdpr-info.eu/art-89-gdpr/),
Norwegian Personal Data Act §17). Here is how each applies to a
typical CMI project:

**Right to information**
([Articles 13](https://gdpr-info.eu/art-13-gdpr/)–[14](https://gdpr-info.eu/art-14-gdpr/)):
fully applies. This is what the information letter provides. Every
participant must be informed about the research and the data
processing.

**Right of access**
([Article 15](https://gdpr-info.eu/art-15-gdpr/)): applies. A
participant can ask to see the data you hold about them. For interview
data, this means providing their transcript or the relevant portions
of your records.

**Right to rectification**
([Article 16](https://gdpr-info.eu/art-16-gdpr/)): applies. A
participant can ask you to correct factual errors in their data.

**Right to erasure**
([Article 17](https://gdpr-info.eu/art-17-gdpr/)): limited. Under
the public interest basis, you may refuse erasure if deletion would
seriously impair the research
([Article 17(3)(d)](https://gdpr-info.eu/art-17-gdpr/)). CMI's
default, however, is to accommodate deletion requests unless there
is a compelling, documented reason to retain the data.

**Right to data portability**
([Article 20](https://gdpr-info.eu/art-20-gdpr/)): does not apply.
This right exists only when the lawful basis is consent or contract.

**Right to object**
([Article 21](https://gdpr-info.eu/art-21-gdpr/)): limited under
[Article 89(2)](https://gdpr-info.eu/art-89-gdpr/), but researchers
should take objections seriously and accommodate them where possible.

In practice, very few research participants exercise these rights.
But your information letter must describe them, and you should know
how to respond if asked.


## The Sikt notification

### Sikt

The Norwegian Agency for Shared Services in Education and Research.
Among other functions, Sikt provides data protection advisory services
for research. The Sikt notification form is the mechanism by which
research institutions report personal data processing in research
projects.


### Notification (not approval)

The Sikt notification tells Sikt that data processing will take
place and describes how. Sikt's role is to assess whether the
described processing is consistent with data protection requirements
and to advise if they see concerns. They do not approve or reject
research. Filing a notification is a legal requirement, not a
gatekeeping process.


### REK (Regional Committees for Medical and Health Research Ethics)

The ethics committees that review health research under the Health
Research Act. REK approval is required for projects that fall under
this act — clinical trials, health registry studies, and research
that aims to generate new knowledge about health and disease.

Most CMI health-related research is social science research *about*
health (access to services, health policy, wellbeing outcomes) and
does not require REK approval. If you are unsure whether your project
falls under the Health Research Act, consult the RDM Adviser.

REK and Sikt serve different functions: REK reviews research ethics
and design; Sikt assesses data protection compliance. A project that
requires REK approval also needs a Sikt notification.
