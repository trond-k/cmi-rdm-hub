# CMI's Institutional Context
*Trond Kvamme · 7 min read*

## Key takeaways

* **Heterogeneous portfolio, heterogeneous data:** CMI's research portfolio — spanning diverse projects and initiatives across conflict zones, authoritarian contexts, and development settings — generates uniquely valuable yet sensitive data. This creates significant challenges, like protecting participants and researchers from retaliatory harm, navigating fragmented regulatory frameworks, and addressing inequitable partnership dynamics.

* **Opportunities:** However, it also offers important opportunities to elevate marginalised perspectives, question data hierarchies by foregrounding qualitative and contextual data often undervalued relative to quantitative datasets, and build comparability while preserving local meaning. By navigating these tensions, and by curating and making available more of our research data and other knowledge outputs, CMI can challenge hierarchies that privilege certain forms of knowledge over others.

This page provides the analytical foundation for the [principles that guide CMI's approach](data-principles.md). For how these dynamics play out globally, see [The politics of data](politics-of-data.md).

---

## Data diversity and geographic complexity

CMI's research portfolio spans seven groups working across development economics, political science, anthropology, law, and policy advisory, with 92 externally funded projects in 2024 (*CMI Annual Report 2024*). The portfolio's Global South and development research and consultancy focus, combined with work on sensitive topics like authoritarianism, conflict, and migration, generates unique data management challenges that influence how data can be shared and used.

This breadth creates a complex data landscape with highly diverse data types, ranging from randomised controlled trial datasets, household surveys, and agricultural yield measurements, to policy documents, in-depth interviews, ethnographic field notes, visual media, and audio recordings, raising particular concerns about participant identifiability. This variation of projects, collaborations, data and methods, creates a "layered" ecosystem where several organisational and operational challenges co-exist.

## Data management and governance implications

### Sensitivity, compliance, ethics and risks

Together, these characteristics present substantial ethical and operational challenges. Research in conflict-affected and authoritarian contexts creates risks of retaliatory harm if participants are identified. Interview transcripts from diaspora communities, field notes from conflict zones, and ethnographic recordings require careful handling to protect both participants and researchers; identifiability can arise from contextual variables, geospatial clues, or narrative fragments.

Regional regulatory fragmentation compounds these risks, as studies document discordant data protection regimes across EU, African, and other national frameworks, with limited Research Ethics Committee capacity for multi-jurisdictional review (Cengiz, Kabanda, and Moodley 2024; Brand et al. 2022).

However, when applied thoughtfully, the EU's General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) can serve as a vital human-rights-based safeguard in areas where other protections are insufficient. The GDPR embeds privacy as a fundamental right under the EU Charter of Fundamental Rights, applying broadly to personal data and imposing lawful, transparent, and proportionate processing standards (Charter of Fundamental Rights of the EU 2012; EDPB 2021). It also features a research-specific framework: Article 89 allows for processing sensitive data for scientific research if appropriate safeguards, such as data minimisation, pseudonymisation, and "privacy by design," are in place (European Parliament and Council 2016).

For practical guidance on ethics processes, see [Ethics and participant protection](../before/ethics-assessment.md). For data protection specifics, see the [GDPR demystifier](../guides/gdpr-demystifier.md).

### Partnership dynamics and power

**Division of labour:** Multi-national collaborations often centralise data infrastructure in Global North/West institutions while partners located in Global South concentrate on data collection, reproducing inequitable divisions of labour that affect dataset ownership and publication decisions (Jeffery 2013; Bratt et al. 2023; Bothello and Bonfim 2023). Such patterns have the potential to influence CMI's collaborations, particularly where local partners are primarily engaged in data collection roles.

**Asymmetries:** North-South collaborations often display asymmetries, where Northern partners dominate design and authorship, while Southern partners focus on data collection (Jeffery 2013; Bratt et al. 2023). Guidance on equitable partnerships exists but remains unevenly applied (Voller et al. 2022). Within mixed academic-commissioned portfolios, credit, ownership, and access vary: datasets created under time pressure and funder oversight may be governed by bespoke agreements rather than academic norms of openness. Combined with EU compliance requirements and multi-currency accounting, uneven documentation practices can fortify asymmetries (Cengiz, Kabanda, and Moodley 2024; Brand et al. 2022).

**Data sovereignty:** Community-oriented perspectives on data sovereignty, particularly important when working with marginalised populations facing repression or displacement, directly challenge assumptions of frictionless openness and Northern custodianship (Jeffery 2013; Voller et al. 2022). Indigenous and African communitarian ethics underscores that data governance is not merely a technical issue but bound up with agency, reciprocity, and sovereignty: communities must retain meaningful influence over how their knowledge is collected, interpreted, and circulated (Griffiths et al. 2021; Munung, de Vries, and Pratt 2021; Yakubu et al. 2018).

**Ethical frameworks:** Frameworks such as Ubuntu and CARE emphasise collective decision-making and benefit sharing, positioning communities as rights-bearing actors rather than passive data sources (Munung, de Vries, and Pratt 2021). Recent suggestions extend CARE beyond its Indigenous origins, arguing that its principles provide a universal ethical framework for data governance in humanitarian crises and conflict-affected environments. Applied in such contexts, CARE prioritises safety, dignity, and empowerment of vulnerable populations, ensuring that data serves collective welfare rather than external interests (Suchikova and Nazarovets 2025). Complementing these principles, some have challenged the extractive dynamics of research by proposing "research infusion" — a mode of knowledge co-creation grounded in respect, reciprocity, and reflexivity, where communities actively shape research processes and outcomes (Bothello and Bonfim 2025).

**Reciprocity:** In practice, this entails moving beyond extractive fieldwork to embed reciprocal arrangements — through negotiated authorship, participatory analysis, or shared custodianship of knowledge — so that local collaborators and affected groups shape not only research agendas but also the terms of dissemination (Jeffery 2013; Griffiths et al. 2021).

**Qualitative sensitivities:** For qualitative data in particular, where re-use risks misinterpretation and relational harm, recent literature cautions that controlled access, contextualised metadata, and community-led governance may be more appropriate than default openness (DuBois et al. 2023).

For practical guidance on partnership agreements, see [Partnerships and agreements](../guides/contracts-and-agreements.md). For FAIR principles and their relationship to data sovereignty, see [FAIR principles](../guides/fair-principles.md).

## Opportunities for rebalancing

Against this backdrop, the same structural features create opportunities to recalibrate what counts as high-value data and to support comparative analysis that acknowledges, rather than flattens, epistemic heterogeneity.

### Elevating marginalised perspectives: data as representation

Data is not a neutral mirror of reality; it is a medium of representation that amplifies some voices while silencing others. Large, well-resourced datasets often dominate research and policymaking, while perspectives from marginalised groups remain underrepresented or invisible. When disseminated, data actively shapes which stories are told and whose experiences carry weight. **Data is representation**.

For CMI, operating in politically sensitive and fragile contexts, this means adopting an explicit role as a **voice-amplifier** for underrepresented perspectives. CMI's commitment to equal knowledge production and inclusive partnerships aligns with perspectives on community agency and sovereignty in data governance (Carroll et al. 2019; Griffiths et al. 2021). Hybrid dissemination strategies, where controlled-access arrangements coexist with public-facing outputs, allow community voices to be amplified while safeguarding those at risk (Griffiths et al. 2021; Brand et al. 2022; Cengiz, Kabanda, and Moodley 2024).

Anthropological, development, and rights-focused research, anchored in long-term engagement, enables reciprocity, reflexivity, and co-authorship to be embedded directly into data governance. These practices move beyond extractive models of fieldwork, ensuring that communities help shape research agendas and co-produce knowledge (Carroll et al. 2019; Griffiths et al. 2021).

### Hybrid governance beyond open/closed binaries

CMI's work across diverse regulatory environments positions its researchers to develop and refine data management and governance models that move beyond simplistic open/closed binaries. Documentation strategies for multi-site comparative projects can create comparators that preserve local meanings and contextual specificity while still enabling systematic cross-site analysis, countering homogenising tendencies in global development research (Voller et al. 2022).

Hybrid governance involves differentiated access regimes: some materials may be openly available; others released only under controlled conditions; some restricted to metadata publication without underlying data; and still others retained exclusively by local partners. These models are especially relevant in politically sensitive settings, where exposure may endanger participants or collaborators, yet selective visibility remains essential for accountability and policy engagement.

For practical approaches to sharing and archiving, see [Sharing and archiving](../after/sharing-and-archiving.md).

### Innovation under constraint

Finally, the demands of managing sensitive data in conflict-affected or authoritarian contexts can drive methodological innovation, in ways that can inform broader practice. When participant safety, surveillance, and political repression are constant risks, standard approaches to openness and storage are insufficient. In such environments, CMI researchers and their partners can develop techniques for privacy-preserving data management that ensure information is both usable for analysis and protected against misuse.

Secure collaboration methods also emerge from necessity: distributed teams may rely on layered access permissions, minimal data transfers, or in-country custodianship to avoid exposing partners to legal or physical danger. These approaches, while shaped by crisis, have wider application. The same strategies that protect interviewees in authoritarian states can be valuable for safeguarding marginalised groups in other contexts where risks stem from surveillance, commercial data mining, or political harassment.

In this way, contexts of constraint can serve as laboratories for innovation. Solutions designed to protect the most vulnerable, like secure metadata-only publishing, privacy-by-design workflows, and community-controlled archives, can strengthen ethical standards and data security across CMI's entire portfolio. What begins as a necessity in fragile settings can become a model for good practice in less acute but still ethically sensitive research environments.

For guidance on securing data in practice, see [Data security](../guides/data-security.md) and [Storing and securing data](../during/data-storage.md).

## Implications for CMI

CMI's mission requires high-quality, findable, and interpretable data aligned with EU and funder expectations of transparency. At the same time, commissioned work may require strict confidentiality, producing inherent tensions between visibility and protection. Impact depends on **strategic visibility** — balancing openness for peer and policy engagement with confidentiality for participant safety and partner trust.

Equal knowledge production reframes these tensions: co-publication, shared dataset ownership, and inclusive methods help advance CMI's goals but require navigating infrastructural asymmetries. In some cases, community-controlled governance is the ethical prerequisite for visibility, shifting what "sharing" means beyond technical openness.

The institutional challenge is to balance these demands across a heterogeneous portfolio. Portfolio diversity enables CMI to draw connections across contexts and reach different audiences, but it also demands careful governance to ensure that sensitive data remain protected even as they are made analytically useful and publicly relevant. Data sharing aligns with strategic aims when it ensures discoverability and credibility while protecting communities and respecting partner roles. This requires institutional frameworks that treat data management not as a technical add-on but as central to CMI's model of engaged, equitable research in challenging contexts.
