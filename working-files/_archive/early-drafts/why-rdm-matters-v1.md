# Why research data management matters

Research data management is part of doing research well. It is not only about compliance, archiving, or open science. It is also about research quality, participant protection, secure collaboration, and making data understandable and usable over time.

CMI’s research often involves fieldwork, interviews, surveys, international collaboration, commissioned work, and research in politically or socially sensitive settings. In that context, data management is a practical research issue from the start of a project to its closure.

## Why this matters

Research data is one of the main foundations of research quality. If data is poorly organised, weakly documented, stored in insecure ways, or dependent on one person’s memory, the project becomes harder to manage and the research becomes harder to interpret, verify, reuse, or build on.

Good data management helps make research more:

- secure
- understandable
- traceable
- collaborative
- durable

At CMI, this matters especially because projects often involve:

- multiple researchers or partners
- different forms of data, from interview recordings to survey files
- context-specific risks that are not always visible in the file itself
- legal, ethical, contractual, and funder-related requirements
- data that may be difficult to share openly, but still needs to be documented and governed well

## Research data management is more than storage

Research data management does not only mean choosing where files should be stored. It includes the full set of decisions and practices that shape how data is handled across the project lifecycle.

This includes, for example:

- identifying what data the project will create, receive, or reuse
- deciding who should have access to which materials
- documenting how data was collected, processed, or changed
- separating identifying information from core research material where relevant
- planning for retention, deletion, archiving, or sharing
- making sure others can understand the material later

In other words, research data management is what turns a collection of files into research material that is usable, governable, and, where appropriate, shareable.

## What counts as research data at CMI

The practical question is: what materials does your project produce or collect that someone would need in order to understand, verify, or build on your findings? That is your research data, and it needs a management plan.

At CMI, this should be understood broadly. Research data includes not only raw material, but also processed and working files derived from it, and the documentation needed to make it usable. Data are not neutral givens: they are shaped by collection choices, instruments, and context, which is why documentation matters as much as the data files themselves.

Examples of research data at CMI include:

- interview recordings and transcripts
- survey datasets and codebooks
- fieldnotes and observation notes
- photographs, video, audio, and geospatial data
- translation files and coding files
- scripts, tables, and cleaned working datasets
- consent records and participant information
- README files, data dictionaries, and methodological notes

Many practical problems arise in the gaps. A dataset may be well stored but poorly documented. Transcripts may be organised, but translation decisions may not be recorded. A pseudonymised file may exist, but the key file may be stored unsafely. Good data management requires attention to the whole data environment, not only to the main data files.

## Four ways data matters at CMI

At CMI, research data management matters not only because projects need secure storage and clear routines. It also matters because data is never only technical. Data shapes what becomes visible, whose perspectives are preserved, what the institute remembers, and what future research can build on.

### Data is political

Data is not neutral. Decisions about what is collected, how it is described, which materials are retained, and what can be shared all shape how research evidence enters public debate, policy processes, and institutional memory.

At CMI, this matters because projects often work in politically or socially sensitive settings, and because research may be used in governance, advocacy, or commissioned contexts. Good data management therefore supports transparency, accountability, and careful judgement about context, risk, and representation.

### Data is voice

Data does not only record information. It also affects whose experiences and perspectives become visible in research.

At CMI, this matters across a wide range of methods, from surveys and administrative data to interviews, observation, and qualitative field material. Good data management helps ensure that qualitative, contextual, and less standardised forms of material are documented and handled with the same seriousness as more formal datasets.

### Data is memory

Data is part of CMI's institutional memory. If material is lost, poorly documented, or left dependent on one person's folders and decisions, future researchers cannot understand or build on it.

At CMI, this matters because projects often run across time, teams, and partnerships. Good data management helps preserve datasets, transcripts, instruments, codebooks, and methodological notes in ways that support continuity, archiving, and later reuse where appropriate.

### Data is value

Data is one of the main things that connects CMI's different project types. Academic studies, commissioned evaluations, and collaborative field-based projects may differ in purpose and design, but they all produce material that can retain value beyond the original project.

Good data management helps protect that value by making data more understandable, governable, and reusable over time. This is part of how CMI strengthens research quality, institutional continuity, and long-term credibility.

## What good data management helps you do

Good research data management helps researchers and project teams:

- keep track of versions, folders, and workflows
- avoid confusion about which files are authoritative
- reduce unnecessary exposure of sensitive or identifiable material
- support collaboration within and across teams
- make it easier to understand how data was generated and processed
- prepare material for publication, archiving, or controlled sharing
- reduce dependence on informal routines or individual memory
- make project closure easier and safer

In many projects, the first benefit is internal. Better data management usually means less confusion, less duplication, better continuity, and fewer avoidable risks during the project itself.

## The lifecycle approach in this handbook

This handbook uses a lifecycle approach. That means research data management is treated as something that runs through the whole project, not as a one-time requirement.

The main stages are:

1. Frame
2. Fund
3. Plan
4. Collect or acquire
5. Store and secure
6. Process and document
7. Analyse and collaborate
8. Publish
9. Preserve and archive
10. Close and review

This approach reflects how research projects actually work. Important data decisions are made early, often before data collection starts, and they continue throughout the life of the project.

## Key point

At CMI, research data management is about handling data in ways that support:

- research quality
- ethical and legal responsibility
- secure and workable collaboration
- transparency and documentation
- long-term stewardship

It should be practical, proportionate, and adapted to the actual conditions of the project.

## Minimum you should be able to answer

At the start of a project, you should be able to answer these questions:

- What data will the project create, receive, or reuse?
- Will any of it be personal, sensitive, confidential, or difficult to share?
- Who needs access to what?
- How should the data be stored and documented?
- What may later need to be retained, deleted, archived, or shared?

### Quick guide: Identify the data in your project

Use these five questions:

1. What material will the project create, receive, or reuse?
2. Which parts may be personal, sensitive, confidential, or contextually risky?
3. Which files are research data, and which are supporting documentation?
4. Who needs access during the project?
5. What may later need to be shared, archived, retained, or deleted?
